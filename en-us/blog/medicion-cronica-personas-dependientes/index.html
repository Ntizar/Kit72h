Chronic medication with no pharmacy: 2-4 weeks secured (2026)

📅 2026-09-02 · ⏱ 5 min min read

A typical emergency lasts 72 hours; a rare one, weeks. For a household with chronic treatment, the problem isn't the scare on day one: it's day eight, when the box you had left runs out and your local pharmacy is closed or flooded. What follows is pure logistics: paperwork, reserves and rotation so that dependency doesn't turn into an emergency. What to take and how much is decided by each person with their doctor or pharmacist; none of that is covered here.

Step 0: the inventory you haven't done yet

Open the medicine drawer and make a list for every member of the household. Without a list there is no plan: it's the equivalent of not knowing how much water is left after the supply has already been cut off.

  • The treatment name exactly as it appears on the box (not the family nickname), the regimen given by the professional, and which full boxes are left at home.
  • Date of the last prescription and where it is renewed: GP, specialist or the specific pharmacy if it is a compounded product.
  • What the consequences of an interruption are: the prescriber tells you that, so ask at the next check-up and write it down. Missing one day is not the same as a week without treatment, and it's worth knowing before, not during.
  • How each item is stored: some treatments suffer in summer heat or need a fridge, and a July power cut turns a switched-off refrigerator into a countdown.

The 2-4 week reserve, without overbuying

The buffer is built with a system that pharmacies and primary care know about, and you only have to ask for it:

  • Rotation, not stockpiling: collect each refill as far in advance as the system allows and use the reserve at home, so there is always a margin without accumulating expired items. The exact mechanics depend on your treatment: discuss them with your pharmacy.
  • Never open extra packages or keep other people's leftovers: the reserve is your own treatment, in its box, with its date. Swapping pills between family members is a direct route to a dosing error.
  • One week travelling in the evacuation backpack: if your area is evacuated, the reserve in the drawer stays in a building you may not be able to enter for days. What leaves with you decides how you are on Thursday.
  • Support consumables (blood glucose test strips, wound care supplies, catheters, ostomy bags, CPAP masks): they are restocked every month and in a crisis they run out before medicines do. Ask your named nurse for a reasonable figure for your case; don't improvise the number yourself.

Fragile chains: cold, electricity and memory

Some dependencies cannot be solved with boxes in a cupboard:

  • What needs a fridge: a closed refrigerator that is opened little stays cool for 24 to 48 hours; with frozen water bottles as blocks, a little longer. If it drags on, ask your pharmacy which products can tolerate a brief time out of the cold and which cannot: each one is different, and the professional says so with the package leaflet in front of them.
  • What needs electricity (oxygen concentrators, ventilators, pumps): here the reserve is energy. A power bank compatible with the device, a spare battery, and notifying your electricity company and the Protección Civil in your region: many keep registers of customers dependent on life-support equipment to warn them and prioritise their supply during long outages. Ask whether your case is registered, because you don't get on the list automatically.
  • People with cognitive impairment: the risk is not only the pill; it is that routine breaks down and the person stops eating, drinking or taking what they should without realising. Stable schedules, the regimen written in large print and an identification sheet with contacts: in chaos, a disoriented older person with no ID attached is the worst case a rescue team can handle.

The treatment sheet: a single page that fixes almost everything

A laminated sheet in the emergency folder and a photocopy in the backpack:

  • Full name, date of birth and phone number of the person receiving care and of their caregiver.
  • Active treatments with the regimen written by the professional, and what each one is for: that way whoever covers for you one day understands it.
  • Contacts: prescriber, named nurse, usual pharmacy, health centre, responsible family member.
  • Allergies and intolerances, and personal warning signs that mean you should seek medical help without hesitation.
  • Update date at the top: every medical check-up is a run through this sheet. A two-year-old piece of paper is worse than none: it gives false confidence.

The drill almost nobody does

Once a year, with the list in front of you: expiry dates, device batteries, waterproof case intact, actual stock in the backpack and not just in theory. And if care is shared among several people — siblings, children who live far away, home help —, roles in writing: who grabs the backpack, who grabs the person, who notifies the centre. Shared care without a plan fails exactly on the day when each person thought it was the other's turn.

Caregiver logistics are covered in the guide for older people and dependent persons, and the medication reserve fits into the medicine cabinet of the home kit. If your dependency is power-based, review the power outage kit before next summer.
