How to heat your home without electricity in winter: 6 methods that work (2026)

📅 2026-11-16 · ⏱ 7 min min read

When the power goes out in the middle of winter, the first problem isn't the darkness or a dead phone battery: it's the cold coming into your home. A home without heating can lose between 2 and 4 °C per hour on a Spanish winter night. In 6 hours, it goes from 18 °C to 6 °C — a temperature that already poses a risk of hypothermia for children and older adults.

This winter (2026-2027), the State Meteorological Agency (AEMET) forecasts below-average temperatures across most of the peninsula, with episodes of intense cold and isolated snow. Protección Civil recommends being prepared: a winter blackout can become a risky situation within a few hours.

Before buying anything: 4 insulation techniques (0 €)

The cheapest and most effective method is to stop heat from escaping. If you have gas, electric, or pellet heating and it's working, these techniques multiply its efficiency. If you have nothing, they are your only line of defense:

1. Close off rooms you don't use

Less volume = less heat to maintain. Close doors and, if a room has drafts from the windows, temporarily seal it with rolled-up towels at the threshold. According to the IDAE (Institute for Energy Diversification and Saving), closing off an unheated room creates an air pocket that acts as extra insulation, reducing heat loss by 15-20%.

2. Insulate the windows

Windows account for up to 25% of heat loss in an average Spanish home (IDAE data). If you have blinds, close them at night — the air gap between the glass and the blind is a natural insulator. An extra trick: place cardboard or newspaper on the inside of the window fixed with temporary tape. It isn't pretty, but it raises the perceived temperature by 2-3 °C.

3. Cover the floor

The floor loses between 10% and 15% of the heat. Thick rugs, old blankets, or even cardboard boxes underfoot act as a thermal barrier. If you have carpet, clean it — dust reduces its insulating capacity by 10-15%.

4. The three-layer rule

Three thin shirts retain more heat than one thick one: the air trapped between layers is the real insulator. Thick socks are essential — cold feet cool the whole body through circulation. Gloves indoors aren't an exaggeration: you lose significant heat through your hands.

Method 1: Butane heater (the most practical)

The butane (propane) heater is the most widespread method in Spain for emergency heating. A 12 kg cylinder can last between 2 and 3 days with intermittent use at medium power, and the cost per hour is around 0,09-0,11 € (at 0,38 €/kg).

Strengths: immediate ignition, no electricity needed, low cost, widely available cylinders. Weaknesses: produces moisture, requires minimum ventilation and consumes oxygen.

Safety rule: always with a window slightly open (100 cm² of ventilation) and a CO detector. Protección Civil makes it clear: ventilation is not optional.

Method 2: Catalytic heater (the most efficient)

Catalytic technology generates heat without a visible flame through a chemical reaction on a ceramic panel. The result: lower consumption (110-280 g/h compared with 280-305 g/h for traditional heaters), no open flame (safer with children and pets), and more even heat.

The catalytic panel is a consumable that lasts about two winters; replacing it costs around 30-40 €. With 4 hours of daily use, a 12 kg cylinder can last up to two weeks.

Method 3: Fireplace or pellet stove

If you already have a fireplace or pellet stove installed, it's the most natural way to heat several rooms. Modern pellet stoves have an efficiency above 85% (the National Institute of Industrial Technology measures efficiencies between 85-92%).

Limitation: most need electricity to power the hopper and fan. In a blackout, they only work if you have a SAI or a connected power station. Check the blackout kit to see which power stations support this type of stove.

Method 4: Gas stove for heating (emergencies only)

In a pinch, the gas stove (butane or natural gas) can be used to heat a room. Warning: only for brief emergencies (maximum 1-2 hours) and ALWAYS with plenty of ventilation. It is not a heating method — it is an emergency resource.

A gas oven at 200 °C emits approximately 2,000 W of heat. Leaving the door ajar (never closed, risk of gas accumulation) can heat the kitchen by 2 to 4 °C.

Method 5: Thermal blankets and indoor tents

The emergency thermal blanket (the gold or silver ones for 2 €) reflects up to 90% of radiated body heat. It works best wrapped inside a normal blanket — the blanket protects from the wind and the thermal blanket reflects heat.

A small tent (for warmth) inside the home creates a microclimate that can raise the internal temperature by 5 to 8 °C. Mountaineers have been doing this for decades: a small space, isolated from the rest of the home, is warmed by body heat and blankets. Method 6: Power station + electric radiator

A 1,000 Wh power station can power a small oil-filled radiator (1,000 W) for approximately 45-60 minutes. That's not much, but it's enough to keep a room at a reasonable temperature overnight if combined with the insulation techniques above.

The trick is not to use the power station for continuous heating: use it as occasional backup (2-3 hours) while you optimize insulation. The power station in the blackout kit is a supplement, not a replacement.

Warning signs: when to call 112

It's not a matter of comfort, it's a matter of safety. If the indoor temperature drops below 10 °C and there are vulnerable people (children under 6, adults over 65, people with respiratory or cardiovascular conditions), the situation should be treated as an emergency.

The symptoms of mild hypothermia are: uncontrollable shivering, difficulty speaking, clumsy movements. At temperatures below 5 °C, the risk increases exponentially. The 112 Especial app from Protección Civil lets you request help when the line is overloaded — have it downloaded before the crisis hits.

The lesson of the Spanish winter

Recent winters in Spain have shown that preparation costs less than suffering. A cheap butane heater (60-80 €), thermal blankets (2-5 €), and a CO detector (15-25 €) add up to less than 120 € — less than a month of conventional electric heating.

The most important thing isn't the equipment: it's deciding before the power goes out which room you'll stay in, how you'll insulate it, and what heat source you'll use. A decision made in panic, at 10 at night and in the cold, is almost always a bad decision.
