Earthquake in Spain: what to do before, during and after (2026)

📅 2026-09-29 · ⏱ 6 min min read

On Tuesday 18 August 2026, at 10:37, a magnitude 4.8 earthquake 5 kilometres deep struck the province of Granada, with the epicentre north-west of Gójar. That same day there were four tremors of magnitude 4 or above, with aftershocks of 4.3 and 4.2 in Alhendín, and the Junta de Andalucía ordered the preventive evacuation of six blocks in Las Gabias: 371 homes and around 600 people out of their houses while engineers checked pillars and cracks. Three people were slightly injured.

The important thing isn't the magnitude: it's that hundreds of families were left not knowing when they could go back. Earthquakes don't announce themselves, so the only part of the script you control is you — and only if you work on it before the shaking starts. This is the sequence recommended by Civil Protection, with data from the Instituto Geográfico Nacional (IGN).

Spain shakes more than you think

The IGN's National Seismic Network located 5,729 earthquakes on the Spanish mainland during 2025, of which 182 were felt by the population. The total varies a lot from one year to the next — when a seismic series like the Granada one starts, it shoots up — but the pattern is always the same: thousands of tiny movements, a handful you can feel and, now and then, one that really registers.

In 2025 there were 7 earthquakes above magnitude 4 and none reached 6: almost all of them go unnoticed. The problem is the one that doesn't.

Why it shakes and why nobody can predict it

Spain sits on the edge of the Eurasian plate where it collides with the African plate, along the Azores-Gibraltar-Tunisia fracture zone; that's how Civil Protection's seismic risk guide puts it, and it is just as blunt about what we can't do: "There is currently no method capable of predicting the time, place and magnitude of an earthquake". There is no app and no advance notice. The only thing that works is being ready when it arrives without warning.

What has been worked out is the building. Spain's seismic-resistant construction standard, NCSE-02 (Real Decreto 997/2002), sets out as its purpose preventing the loss of human life and reducing economic damage. Not all buildings are in the same situation: that's why, in Las Gabias, engineers inspected each block before authorising the return, and why the Patronato de la Alhambra closed the Nasrid Palaces preventively that afternoon. No house is "safe" or "dangerous" in the abstract: it gets inspected and decided with the report in front of you.

Before: five things for this week (they cost 0 €)

  • Secure the bookcase, the TV and the tall wardrobes to the wall. In an earthquake, what injures you is almost never the floor: it's what falls off it.
  • Find the gas shut-off valve and check that it turns. After the shaking you'll have seconds and your hands will be trembling. If you smell gas, don't switch anything on: no light switches, no matches.
  • Closed shoes and a torch next to the bed, tied on or under the mattress: broken glass isn't visible in the dark.
  • A written family agreement: a meeting point and a contact outside the province, because local lines get overloaded first. You'll find it in the family emergency plan.
  • Documents, first-aid kit and backpack near the door: the emergency folder with ID, deeds and insurance policy, the medication in the first-aid kit and the backpack already packed.

During: you have between five and thirty seconds

The official recommendation fits into three verbs: drop, cover and hold on. Get on all fours, get under a sturdy table and hold on to it: it will move with you and protect you from whatever falls. Three don'ts prevent the rest of the injuries:

  • Don't run out to the stairwell and don't use the lift: stay where you are until the shaking stops.
  • Don't run down into the street from upper floors. Stay away from windows, mirrors, shelves and façades. If you're already outside, head for an open space away from cornices and cables.
  • If you're in a car, stop in an open spot: not under bridges, not next to streetlights, not under cables.

After: the part almost everyone forgets

  • Shoes on before you set foot on the floor. Just awake and in the dark, the glass is on the floor and you can't see it.
  • Don't go back inside until it's been checked. In Granada the evacuations were preventive, because of cracks that grew between one inspection and the next; if you see new cracks or a door that no longer closes, don't go in and report it.
  • Expect aftershocks in the hours that follow: nobody can tell you which ones or when, so the rule is the same as at the start.
  • Reliable information, not rumours. ES-Alert is the "reverse 112" that Civil Protection sends via Cell Broadcast and its Civil Protection Alert level can't be switched off on your phone; a battery radio does the rest, as covered in communicating without a mobile network and official resources on your phone.
  • Document before you move anything: photos of cracks and fallen objects for your insurer and the council: no evidence, no claim.

Three myths that come back with every seismic series

"It doesn't really happen here." On 11 May 2011 Lorca shook at magnitude 5.1 and intensity VII: nine dead and more than 300 injured. On 25 December 1884 the Arenas del Rey earthquake (Granada) destroyed some 4,400 houses and damaged another 13,000, with around 800 fatalities according to the geophysics institute of the University of Granada. What kills isn't the magnitude: it's distance, the ground and the state of the building.

"You can know when the next one will come." No: that's the literal wording from Civil Protection. Be wary of anyone announcing dates.

"Magnitude explains everything." Magnitude measures the energy released and doesn't change as you move away from the epicentre; intensity — the European EMS scale, from I to XII — measures how it was felt at a specific place and drops with distance. A 4.8 five kilometres under the outskirts of a provincial capital is felt across the whole metropolitan area; the same 4.8 far away is barely noticed.

Your list for this week

  1. Screws and a driver: TV and bookcase strapped to the wall.
  2. Find the gas shut-off valve and test it.
  3. Closed shoes and a torch next to the bed.
  4. Write down the family agreement: meeting point and a contact outside the province.
  5. Update the document folder, the medication and the date on the backpack.
  6. Keep the battery radio charged and find the official warning channels.

The item-by-item detail is in the earthquake kit and in the evacuation backpack; if you're only going to put one together, make it the basic 72-hour kit, the one that keeps you going when the building holds but there's no power, water or gas.
