Earthquake in Spain: what to do before, during and after (2026)

📅 2026-09-29 · ⏱ 6 min min read

On Tuesday, 18 August 2026, at 10:37, a magnitude 4.8 earthquake at a depth of 5 kilometers struck the province of Granada, with its epicenter northwest of Gójar. That same day there were four earthquakes of magnitude 4 or higher, with aftershocks of 4.3 and 4.2 in Alhendín, and the Junta de Andalucía ordered the preventive evacuation of six blocks in Las Gabias: 371 homes and around 600 people out of their homes while technicians inspected pillars and cracks. Three people sustained minor injuries.

The important thing is not the magnitude: it is that hundreds of families were left not knowing when they could return. Earthquakes do not announce themselves, so the only part of the script you control is you, and only if you work on it before the shaking starts. This is the sequence recommended by Civil Protection, with data from the Instituto Geográfico Nacional (IGN).

Spain has more earthquakes than you think

The IGN's National Seismic Network located 5,729 earthquakes on the Peninsula during 2025, of which 182 were felt by the population. The total varies greatly from one year to another —when a seismic series such as the one in Granada begins, it shoots up— but the pattern is always the same: thousands of tiny movements, a handful that are perceptible and, from time to time, one that is really felt.

In 2025 there were 7 earthquakes above magnitude 4 and none reached 6: almost all go unnoticed. The problem is the one that does not.

Why the ground shakes and why no one can predict it

Spain lies on the edge of the Eurasian plate where it collides with the African plate, over the Azores-Gibraltar-Tunisia fracture; this is explained in the Civil Protection seismic risk guide, which is equally blunt about what we cannot do: “There is currently no method capable of predicting the time, place and magnitude of an earthquake.” There is no app or advance warning. The only thing that works is being prepared when it arrives without warning.

What is calculated is the building. The Spanish seismic-resistant construction standard, NCSE-02 (Royal Decree 997/2002), sets as its purpose avoiding loss of human life and reducing economic damage. Not all buildings are in the same situation: that is why, in Las Gabias, technicians inspected each block before authorizing return, and why the Patronato de la Alhambra preventively closed the Nasrid palaces that afternoon. No house is “safe” or “dangerous” in the abstract: it is inspected and decided with the report in hand.

Before: five things to do this week (they cost 0 €)

  • Secure the shelving, the TV and the tall wardrobes to the wall. In an earthquake, injuries almost never come from the ground: they come from what falls.
  • Locate the gas shut-off valve and check that it turns. After the shaking you will have seconds, and your hands will be trembling. If you smell gas, do not turn anything on: no switches, no matches.
  • Closed-toe shoes and a flashlight next to the bed, tied on or under the mattress: broken glass is not visible in the dark.
  • Written family agreement: meeting point and a contact outside the province, because local lines become saturated first. It is in family emergency plan.
  • Documents, first-aid kit and backpack near the door: the emergency folder with DNI, deeds and policy, the medication from the emergency first-aid kit and the backpack already packed.

During: you have between five and thirty seconds

The official recommendation fits into three verbs: drop, cover and hold on. Get on all fours, get under a sturdy table and hold on to the furniture: it will move with you and protect you from what falls. Three prohibitions prevent the rest of the injuries:

  • Do not run out to the stairs or use the elevator: stay where you are until the shaking stops.
  • Do not run out into the street from upper floors. Stay away from windows, mirrors, shelves and façades. If you are already in the street, go to an open space away from cornices and cables.
  • If you are driving, stop in an open place: not under bridges, not next to streetlights, not under cables.

After: the part almost everyone forgets

  • Before stepping on the floor, put on shoes. Just after getting up and in the dark, glass is on the floor and cannot be seen.
  • Do not go back inside until it has been inspected. In Granada the evacuations were preventive because of cracks that grew between one inspection and the next; if you see new cracks or a door that no longer closes, do not go in and report it.
  • Expect aftershocks in the following hours: no one can tell you which ones or when, so the rule is the same as at the beginning.
  • Reliable information, not rumors. ES-Alert is the “reverse 112” issued by Civil Protection via Cell Broadcast, and its Civil Protection Alert level cannot be disabled on your mobile phone; the battery-powered radio does the rest, as covered in communicating without a mobile network and official resources on your mobile.
  • Document before moving anything: photos of cracks and fallen objects for the insurer and the city council: without evidence there is no case file.

Three myths that come back with every seismic series

“Nothing really happens here.” On 11 May 2011 Lorca shook with magnitude 5.1 and intensity VII: nine dead and more than 300 injured. On 25 December 1884 the Arenas del Rey earthquake (Granada) destroyed some 4,400 houses and left damage in another 13,000, with around 800 fatalities according to the geophysics institute of the University of Granada. What kills is not the magnitude: it is the distance, the ground and the condition of the building.

“You can know when the next one will come.” No: that is the literal wording from Civil Protection. Be wary of anyone announcing dates.

“Magnitude explains everything.” Magnitude measures the energy released and does not change as you move away from the epicenter; intensity —the European EMS scale, from I to XII— measures how it was felt at a specific place and decreases with distance. A 4.8 at 5 kilometers below the outskirts of a capital city is felt across the entire metropolitan area; the same 4.8 far away is barely perceived.

Your list for this week

  1. Screws and fixing key: TV and shelving secured to the wall.
  2. Locate the gas shut-off valve and test it.
  3. Closed-toe shoes and a flashlight next to the bed.
  4. Write the family agreement: meeting point and contact outside the province.
  5. Update the document folder, the medication and the date on the backpack.
  6. Keep the battery-powered radio charged and locate the official warning channels.

The item-by-item detail is in the earthquake kit and in the evacuation backpack; if you are only going to assemble one, let it be the basic 72-hour kit, the one that supports you when the building holds but there is no power, water or gas.
