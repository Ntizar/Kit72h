HydraPak Seeker 3L and 10 L: the collapsible water container that truly deserves a place in your kit

There comes a point when putting together an emergency kit stops being a checklist and becomes a space problem. Food fits. The radio fits. The flashlight fits. And then water comes along, and everything breaks: three liters per person per day is nine liters for 72 hours, which in 1.5-liter bottles means six bottles per person. Six bottles that take up space, weigh you down, rattle with every step and, when empty, still take up as much room as when full.

The 3-liter HydraPak Seeker tackles that problem from the only angle that works: it removes the container from the problem. It is a 92-gram flexible reservoir that fills when there is water and flattens until it fits in a pocket when there isn't. It is not a hiker's gadget: it is the piece that turns “I carry water” into “I can carry water and, when it runs out, I can go get more.”

What exactly it is (with the manufacturer's spec sheet figures)

The Seeker is a water bag, not a bottle. That changes everything else. These are the figures HydraPak provides and that are worth having on hand before deciding:

  • Capacity: 3 liters (100 ounces).
  • Empty weight: 92 grams. Less than a phone.
  • Dimensions when full: 37 × 14 cm, flat. It fits upright in the side pocket of almost any backpack.
  • Material: TPU with radio-frequency-welded seams, plus polypropylene and nylon in the handle and reinforcements. BPA- and PVC-free.
  • Opening: 42 mm, with a screw cap attached to the bag (so it won't get lost) and a coated canvas handle for pouring.
  • Compatibility: 42 mm screw-on filters — the Katadyn BeFree and the brand's own filter cap. With the Plug-N-Play cap and a 28 mm adapter (sold separately), it can be used as a bladder with a hose.
  • Temperature: it can be frozen and accepts hot water up to 60 °C. It is not a thermos and will not withstand boiling water.
  • Extras: two side attachment points for hanging it or setting up gravity filtration, an external capacity gauge and a carrying bag included.

Reference price: between €20 and €30 depending on finish and store. For what it solves, it is one of the most cost-effective purchases in the kit.

Why 3 liters is the number that fits

Spanish self-protection guidelines use two figures: 1.5 liters per person per day for drinking only and 3 liters per person per day if you also cook with that water. Over 72 hours, that is between 4.5 and 9 liters per person. A 3-liter reservoir does not cover the full scenario, and that is exactly what makes it useful: it covers one full day for one person with a margin, or one day of drinking for two.

That size fits the real logic of a 72-hour kit, which is not “carry all the water” but carry water for the critical stretch and have a way to replenish it. The large reserve stays at home, in 8-liter jerrycans you are not going to move. The Seeker is the part that goes out with you: to the car, to the shelter, to the distribution point, to the public fountain that is still working when the tap at home is not.

If you want the full calculation of how much water a household needs, it is set out in the guide to how much water to store and how to store it. Here the question is different: how do you move it?

The five reasons it beats a jerrycan

1. Empty, it takes up no space. This is the main reason and it does not need much explaining: an empty 5-liter jerrycan is still a 5-liter jerrycan. The empty Seeker folds, flattens and goes into its bag, which takes up less room than a wallet. In an evacuation backpack, where every liter of volume competes with clothing and the first-aid kit, that space is gold.

2. It adapts to the space available, not the other way around. Because it is not rigid, it molds to the shape of the pack, fits in a side pocket, hangs from the carry handle and does not create that uncomfortable pressure on your back that a rigid bottle creates when the backpack is full. In a car trunk the same applies: it goes into any gap between boxes.

3. It turns the source into a tap. Here is what almost no one values until they use it: the 42 mm cap accepts screw-on filters. You fill the bag in a river, an irrigation ditch or a questionable tank, screw on the filter and drink directly or by gravity by hanging it from the attachment points. It is the difference between carrying water and being able to make it. The filters that fit without an adapter are 42 mm screw-on ones, such as the Katadyn BeFree. If the scenario is a flood with the supply cut off or contaminated, that capability stops being a mountain extra and becomes the center of the plan. More on this in the comparison of water purifiers for emergencies.

4. It is useful for more than drinking. Three liters of hot water (up to 60 °C) inside a sleeping bag is a full-body heat pack. Frozen, it is a block of ice that keeps a cooler or a food box cold. With the cap closed, it works as an emergency shower hung from a branch. And when you get home, it can be used to wash dishes during a blackout without using up the drinking reserve. A jerrycan does one thing; this does five.

5. It takes abuse. TPU with radio-frequency-welded seams is the same material used by high-end hydration bladders. It is not a disposable plastic bag: it can be frozen, filled with hot water, stepped on and stored wet without bursting. The brand offers a lifetime warranty against manufacturing defects, which for a €25 product says quite a lot about what they expect from it.

Where it falls short (and how to fix it)

No tool is perfect, and this one less than others. What you need to know before buying it:

  • At first it tastes of plastic. This is the first thing people who use it say, and it is true: new TPU leaves a taste in the first fills. It is fixed with two washes of warm water with a little baking soda, leaving it full for a couple of hours and drying it open. After that it disappears. Anyone who does not wash it before the first use will regret it at the worst moment.
  • Full, it does not stand on its own. It is flexible: if you leave it upright on the table, it falls over. It is not a real problem (you hang it from the handle or lean it against the backpack), but if you were expecting a bottle that stands upright, this is not it.
  • It is not for drinking on the move. Without the Plug-N-Play cap and the 28 mm adapter, you have to stop, open it and pour. If your plan is to run with it, buy a bladder with a hose. If your plan is an emergency kit, this detail is irrelevant.
  • The 42 mm cap does not fit 28 mm filters without an adapter. Before buying the filter, check the thread: Katadyn BeFree and HydraPak's own caps yes; many hand pumps no.
  • It is not a storage container. Leaving water inside for weeks is asking the material to get lazy: it picks up taste and grows biofilm. Empty it, dry it open and store it.
  • In a long blackout, three liters run out. It is a transport reservoir, not the reserve. The reserve is the jerrycans at home. Anyone who confuses the two will come up short on the second day.

How I set it up in a 72-hour kit

There is no need to complicate it. This is the setup that makes sense, from smallest to largest:

  1. At home: two 8-liter water jerrycans per person, rotated every six months, plus a water purifier. That is the store and is not touched except in an emergency.
  2. In the evacuation backpack: the empty Seeker (92 g is unnoticeable) and a 42 mm screw-on filter. Total committed weight: less than 200 g, plus the ability to drink from any reasonably clean source.
  3. In the car: a second empty Seeker in the glove compartment or under the seat, with two liters of reserve in PET bottles that are rotated. The reservoir is for refilling at a gas station or a fountain when things drag on.
  4. The routine: before each risk season (DANA season in autumn, wildfire season in summer), check it: dry bag, clean cap, filter within its service life, and a fill test to rule out pinholes.

If you are building the kit from scratch, the correct order is water first and equipment afterward. The bigger brother: the 10-liter Seeker

If your problem is not moving water but keeping it still in one place, the same bag exists in 10 liters. It is 261 grams empty and 49 cm tall, and it comes with a Plug-N-Play cap and a camp tap (Camp Tap): you hang it from a beam, a door frame or the tent fly and serve water with one hand, without lifting anything. That detail is what separates it from a jerrycan: a jerrycan has to be tilted, while this stays hanging and you just open the tap.

The numbers match the official recommendation. Ten liters is almost exactly the three liters per person per day that the guidelines call for over 72 hours: for one person it covers all three days, and for a family of three it covers the first day without touching the rest of the reserve. Full, it weighs 10 kilos, so it stays where you put it; empty, it takes up less than a folder and, well dried, it does not pick up odors between emergencies.

It can be frozen, accepts hot water up to 60 °C, is made of BPA- and PVC-free TPU and shares the range's filter compatibility. In an apartment, its natural place is the back of a closet: fill it on the day of the warning, hang it in the kitchen or bathroom and empty it when the warning passes.

Who it is for and who it is not

Buy it if: you have or are putting together a 72-hour kit, an evacuation backpack, a car kit or a mountain kit; you live in a flood, wildfire or supply-cutoff area; or you simply want to be able to take water from a source without carrying bottles. For €20–€30 it has one of the best weight-to-usefulness ratios in the entire kit.

Do not buy it if: you are looking to store water at home (you need rigid jerrycans), you want to drink without stopping while running (you need a bladder with a hose), or you expect a bottle that stands upright on the table. Also not if the plan is to boil water inside it: it is not a pot.

The verdict

The Seeker 3L is not the flashiest product in an emergency kit and is probably the one you will use most often when there is no emergency at all: a hike, a beach day, a car trip, refilling at a fountain. That is exactly the argument in its favor. A kit that is only used in catastrophes rusts away in the closet; one that leaves the house now and then gets known, tested and kept at hand on the day it is needed.
