The kit C. Tangana would buy to save Spanish music (2026)

📅 2026-11-04 · ⏱ 4 min min read

C. Tangana filled stadiums singing that «el madrileño» doesn’t give up. But there is one thing that neither he nor any artist can beat by sheer willpower: a blackout. On 29-O we saw it on a national scale — the power, coverage and access went out — and what began as an anecdote ended up being the best demonstration of why Spanish music literally depends on a plug.

Think about it: a tour is a truckload of amplifiers that need power in every venue. The studio is a tower of machines that can’t take a power outage in the middle of a take. And the entire business — streaming, merchandising, tickets — lives in the cloud, which turns out to be in a datacenter that does have generators, because someone remembered to buy them. This article is an editorial exercise: if “CT” called us tomorrow and said “don’t let the music stop”, this is what we’d put in the two bags — the ones from the brand photo, you know.

Minute 0: the power goes out in the middle of a mix

The first one hit isn’t the concert, it’s the studio. A 200-millisecond outage erases the session, fries a power supply and shuts off the router right when you were uploading the master. The first thing an energy professional buys isn’t a generator: it’s a SAI (the classic “UPS”), a bridge battery that gives 10–20 minutes to save, close and shut down without drama. It’s the cheapest and most cost-effective item on the whole list: a 600–900 VA SAI covers the router, ONT and the mixing computer for the price of a dinner.

And for what can’t tolerate even a 10-minute interruption — the laptop where Ableton lives — a 20,000 mAh power bank with AC output keeps the session alive all night. It’s not poetry: it’s the difference between losing a take and losing the afternoon.

Hour 2: tonight’s concert can’t be canceled

Here the world changes: 3,000 ticket holders aren’t going home because the power grid had a bad day. A small stage can be kept going with a 300–500 Wh portable solar generator: session sound, monitors, backline lighting and the crew’s phones. It won’t run a stadium PA system — that’s a different league — but it saves the acoustic set, the small room and the masterclass.

The formula used by those who know is combined: the battery stores, the 60–100 W folding solar panel recharges silently (the neighbors appreciate that it isn’t a gasoline generator at 2 in the morning), and if things drag on, a 100 Ah LiFePO4 battery multiplies the runtime tenfold. It’s the same architecture we recommend in any electricity kit, except here we load it with meaning: without energy there is no culture.

Hour 6: the city is dark and the team needs to be organized

A long blackout turns any tour crew into an emergency team. That’s where the devices nobody would associate with music come in, and they save the day:

  • Seeing in the dark without killing the battery: a hands-free LED headlamp for loading the van, and a rechargeable area light for the backline.
  • Knowing what’s happening: if data goes down, a battery-powered FM/AM radio is the only channel still broadcasting (and yes, SER would still be reporting the news with or without streaming).
  • Coordinating without coverage: a pair of PMR446 walkie-talkies between FOH and stage saves half an hour of shouting across an empty venue.
  • The beers (and the ham): a portable cooler isn’t a whim — in an emergency, preserving is part of the plan.

The lesson the blackout leaves behind

C. Tangana hasn’t asked for this (note: this is an editorial imagining, not a sponsorship — as Affiliates we only earn if you buy through the links, and the artist hasn’t seen this article). But the underlying idea is his almost without knowing it: doing things right, without depending on someone else to fix them for you. This country’s power grid holds up as well as it holds up; your studio, venue or next live show’s gear can hold up much longer if you decide before the next 29-O.
