Best portable gas cooker for power outages (2026): 5 models compared

Why a portable gas stove is essential when the power goes out

When the power goes out, the first impulse is to open the fridge. But frozen food thaws within hours, and canned food needs cooking to be safe. The portable gas stove is the only tool that heats water, cooks canned food and prepares hot meals without relying on any plug, battery or grid connection.

Civil Protection recommends that every household keep supplies for at least 72 hours, and in winter that autonomy is not much use if you cannot heat water for tea, coffee, soup or pasta. A three-day outage with no way to cook turns into a hydration and malnutrition crisis within 48 hours.

Camping gas stoves used for hiking are also perfect for domestic emergencies. They are compact, inexpensive, run on butane or LPG cylinders available at any petrol station and supermarket, and heat as well as a conventional kitchen burner.

1. Coleman K2 Plus: the most versatile for emergency cooking

The Coleman K2 Plus is the most balanced model for household use during an outage. Its flame-control system lets you go from a quick boil to a slow simmer with the same control as a conventional gas stove. The foldable grate attaches to the 220 g cylinder and can support pots weighing up to 3 kg.

Its most notable feature is its size: it fits in the 72-hour kit backpack (4.5 × 30 × 30 cm folded) and weighs only 500 g with the cylinder included. For a family that shares the K2 with the butane heater for heating, a 220 g cylinder lasts approximately 5–6 hours of intermittent cooking — enough for two meals over a couple of days.

It includes a piezo-electric ignition system (no lighter or matches needed) and a built-in wind shield that improves outdoor efficiency by 30%. It works perfectly indoors, although it is recommended to ventilate the kitchen for at least two minutes every 30 minutes of use.

The CombiCook combines a gas burner on the left with a heating plate (griddle type) on the right. Both run on the same butane cylinder, which allows you to cook two things at once: boil water for pasta and heat canned food on the plate at the same time.

This dual function is key in an outage where cooking time becomes a scarce resource. With the K2 Plus, a family of four has to cook in two batches; with the CombiCook, both meals are prepared in a single round. In a prolonged outage, that time efficiency translates into lower gas consumption and greater response capacity.

Its weight (1.3 kg) makes it a little bulkier than the K2, but it still fits in a medium emergency backpack. Its price (~55 €) is 50% higher than the K2, but the extra functionality justifies the investment for families with small children who need different preparations at the same time.

3. Bonaverde Camping: the most economical option

The Bonaverde is a basic stove that uses a butane cartridge (the type of cylindrical canister that screws on top) with 3,000 W of raw power. It has no precise flame regulator or wind shield, but it boils one litre of water in 2.5 minutes — the same as an electric kettle, but without a plug.

At ~22 €, it is the cheapest stove in this comparison, and for occasional emergency use it is more than sufficient. The weak point is its consumption: at maximum power it uses about 200 g of gas per hour, which means only about 3–4 hours of cooking with one cartridge. For a three-day outage, you would need at least four spare cartridges.

It also lacks a stable base for heavy pots: it is recommended not to exceed 2 kg of weight on the grate. If your family prepares heavy soups and stews, the Bonaverde falls short in a prolonged emergency.

4. Esbit HSB-1: the ultralight tablet stove

The Esbit HSB-1 does not use gas: it burns solid fuel tablets (hexamethylenetetramine) sold in packs of 200 units for ~15 €. Each tablet lasts 25–30 minutes and heats water for coffee, tea or instant soup.

Its advantage is the total absence of an open flame (it burns without a spark, at low temperature) and its absurdly low weight: 250 g without fuel, it fits in a pocket. It is ideal as a secondary stove for heating drinks, preparing dehydrated soups or using water purification tablets in a power outage scenario where hydration is the priority.

But it is not suitable for cooking canned food, boiling large amounts of water or preparing dishes that require prolonged cooking. Its use is limited to "ready-to-eat" preparations or simple heating. For a complete food emergency, it should be accompanied by a more powerful gas stove.

5. Kampa Tarn 2.1: a balance between power and safety

The Kampa Tarn 2.1 is a butane cartridge stove with 2,200 W, a precise flame regulator and a stable three-leg base that makes it very safe on uneven surfaces (camping tables, kitchen countertops with vibration). Its automatic shut-off system cuts off the gas when the cartridge is disconnected, an extra safety feature in domestic environments.

At ~30 € and 450 g, it occupies a middle point between the Bonaverde (cheaper but less safe) and the Coleman K2 (more expensive but more adjustable). It is the recommended option for those who prioritise stability and control over maximum power.

Safety rules for cooking without electricity

Portable gas stoves are safe when used correctly. These rules are non-negotiable:

  • Always in ventilated areas: even if the stove is inside the house (kitchen or dining room), open a window for at least two minutes every 30 minutes of use. Combustion consumes oxygen and produces CO₂.
  • Never use the stove in an enclosed space: without ventilation, carbon monoxide (CO) accumulates without you noticing. A CO detector is mandatory (such as the one in the power outage kit).
  • Secure the surface: place the stove on a flat, stable base. Uneven surfaces (camping tables) require a heat-resistant metal or ceramic base.
  • Keep cylinders away from heat: do not store spare cylinders near the butane heater or other heat sources. Heat expands them and can cause leaks.
  • Do not leave the stove unattended: even if it has piezo-electric ignition and automatic shut-off, the flame should never be left alone.
  • Keep the fire extinguisher nearby: a dry powder fire extinguisher (ABC type) less than two metres from the cooking area is the only fire protection if something goes wrong.

Gas vs electricity: which is more practical in a power outage?

Induction stoves are more efficient (90% of energy is converted into heat, compared with 55% for gas), but they require electricity. A 1,000 Wh power station can power a 1,500 W induction burner for approximately 40 minutes before needing a recharge. A three-day outage becomes a race against time.

Gas, by contrast, does not need recharging. A 220 g cylinder provides ~8–9 kWh of thermal energy (the calorific value of butane is ~10.3 kWh/kg). With two cylinders, you have enough to cook three meals a day for three full days, without relying on any plug or battery.

For a short outage (one day), the power station may be enough. For any emergency that lasts longer, gas is the most practical and predictable option.

Final verdict

The best portable gas stove for a 2026 power outage is the Coleman K2 Plus: precise flame control, extreme portability (500 g), automatic ignition and moderate consumption. At ~35 € it is accessible for any household, and two 220 g cylinders are enough to cover three days of hot meals.

If your priority is cooking for a family of four and you want to maximise efficiency, the Coleman CombiCook (plate + burner at the same time) is worth the extra investment. For tight budgets, the Bonaverde offers the same power for half the price.

And remember: a stove is useless without fuel. If you already know that cold weather is frequent in winter, include two extra butane cylinders in your cold/winter kit, along with the heater and thermal blankets.
