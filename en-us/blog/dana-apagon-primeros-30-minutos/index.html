DANA or blackout: the first 30 minutes, minute by minute (2026)

📅 2026-08-31 · ⏱ 8 min min read

In an emergency, the first thirty minutes decide almost everything: what information you have, where you are and what resources you have left. Civil Protection reduces it to three actions — get informed, secure your home and decide — and the European Commission has insisted since its Preparedness Union Strategy that every household should be able to sustain itself for at least 72 hours on its own while help arrives. This is that sequence, stage by stage, with the mistakes that recur in every DANA warning and every power cut.

If you take away only one idea: the first ten minutes are for information, not action. Almost all avoidable damage in the first half hour — a car swept away in a garage, a television burned out just as the power comes back, a call to 112 to ask something the radio is answering — comes from skipping that order.

Minute 0–10: get informed and communicate without overwhelming the network

When the power goes out or the warning arrives, the reflex is to call and post. Both make the situation worse:

  • 112 is for emergencies involving people at risk: injured people, people trapped, fire, water rising inside an occupied home. To find out whether the warning affects you or why the power is out, there are much faster channels.
  • Messages before calls: WhatsApp or SMS use far less of the network and can be sent even when voice lines are overloaded. An “I’m OK” by message is almost always enough.
  • Battery-powered or hand-crank radio: FM is the only source that stays alive when the mobile network goes down, and it is where the authorities give instructions by voice. A hand-crank and solar emergency radio costs little and depends on neither the network nor the power.
  • Look at the scope, not just the alert: a warning is not the same across the whole territory. Cross-check what you hear with AEMET's weather warnings and with information from your town council or local Civil Protection.

If you receive an emergency message with an alarm on your mobile — the ES-Alert system that Spanish Civil Protection uses to alert the population by phone — it is not a hoax or a mistake: it is the same warning given by radio and public address systems. And if the power cut affects only your building entrance or your street, report it to the building; if there is no power across the whole neighbourhood and you see people in the street, it is a distribution cut and you should wait, without inventing diagnoses. In the 28 April 2025 blackout, confusion about the scope was exactly what fed the wave of hoaxes: the lessons from that day still apply to either scenario.

Everything you need to have located before it is needed — numbers, stations, contacts and where your documents are — is organised in official emergency resources and in the household emergency folder.

Minute 10–20: secure the home (DANA script, blackout script)

Here the path splits.

If water is coming (DANA or flooding)

  • Get out of the basement, garage and ground floors and take upstairs anything that can get wet: documents, footwear, pictures, keys, chargers and medication.
  • Do not go down to get the car. Thirty centimetres of water is enough to sweep a vehicle away, and the garage is the most dangerous place in the building when water comes in.
  • If water enters the home and you can do so without getting wet, turn off the power at the electrical panel and do not use the elevator or any connected appliance.
  • Do not cross streams, fords, tunnels or flooded streets, on foot or by car: the current is much stronger than it looks and you cannot see the bottom.
  • Close shutters and windows, and keep papers you cannot replace in a waterproof bag.

The scenario kit is in DANA kit; the exit plan, in evacuation kit; and what to do when the water recedes and you return home — mud, damp, insurance — in returning home after a flood.

If the power goes out (blackout)

  • Disconnect sensitive equipment — television, computer, audio equipment: the surge that comes when the supply is restored is what burns out electronics. Leave one switch on with a small lamp: it will let you know when the power is back.
  • Do not open the fridge. With the door closed, the cold lasts quite a while; every time you open it, you shorten that.
  • Use a flashlight, not a candle if there are children or pets or if you are going to sleep. And if you use a gas heater, a brazier or a camping stove to get through the cold, ventilate or do not use it while sleeping: carbon monoxide has no smell and is the classic cause of domestic poisoning. A carbon monoxide detector is the genuinely cheap safeguard.
  • Prioritise what you power: mobile and router first, fridge afterwards. A SAI for home keeps the router and equipment running without you noticing, and a solar generator gives hours of power with no noise or fumes; the comparison between the two is in generator vs power station. Minute 20–30: decide whether to stay or go

By default, stay at home if there is no structural risk, no water coming in and no one has ordered you to evacuate: it is the safest place and the one with your resources. Leaving makes sense only if there is an evacuation order, if the building is unsafe or if the water keeps rising.

If you stay:

  • Ration your mobile battery: airplane mode, minimum brightness and data only when you need it. A charged power bank is several full charges; without one, your mobile will die just when you need it most.
  • Take stock of what you have: water, ready-to-eat food, flashlight, batteries, first-aid kit and medication. If something is missing, the basic 72-hour kit and the guide to how much water you need and how to store it give you the exact calculation.
  • Have the family plan decided: meeting points, a contact outside the city and which number to call. That is prepared calmly, not in the 30 minutes: family emergency plan.

If you go, take only what fits in a backpack and can be carried on foot: layers for warmth, water, some food, documents in a waterproof bag, charger and power bank, basic first-aid kit, regular medication, flashlight, whistle and some cash. The full list is in how to assemble your 72-hour kit.

Five mistakes that recur in the first half hour

  1. Using 112 as an information line. Questions are resolved with the radio, the mobile and official channels; 112 is reserved for those in danger.
  2. Going down to the garage to save the car. It is the mistake that causes the most personal injury in flash floods.
  3. Heating with a brazier or gas heater without ventilating, or sleeping with it on. Carbon monoxide slips in without warning; the carbon monoxide detector guide explains it.
  4. Turning everything on at once when the power comes back and opening the fridge every two minutes. Sensitive equipment should be unplugged beforehand and plugged back in gradually.
  5. Believing and sharing any figure or hoax that circulates. In the 2025 blackout, dozens of hoaxes caused real harm; always verify before forwarding.

After the 30 minutes

The first half hour passes and the long part begins: follow only instructions from official sources (112, Civil Protection, local authorities and FM radio), do not touch fallen cables or attempt repairs yourself, and wait for the utility company to restore the supply. If water has come in, check before using anything again; if the outage drags on, food and cold are managed with the guides to eating without power for three days and rotating emergency food.

And when everything returns to normal, restock what you have used: a backpack that is used on the first day and not restocked stops being useful for the next emergency. That is, literally, the last step of the 72-hour plan.

Summary in three lines

First information, then home, decision last: those are the three stages of the first half hour, in that order. In a DANA, do not go down to the garage; in a blackout, do not turn everything on at once and the flashlight comes before the candle. Stay at home unless there is an evacuation order or real risk, and if you leave, leave prepared and with an agreed meeting plan.
