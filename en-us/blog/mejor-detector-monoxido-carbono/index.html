Best carbon monoxide detector: 6 types compared (2026)

📅 2026-10-16 · ⏱ 6 min min read

The cold drops, you light the stove, close the room so the heat doesn't escape, and fall asleep. That scenario—homely, everyday, from any winter in Spain—is the one that most often ends up in the emergency department: carbon monoxide is colorless, odorless and tasteless, it doesn't warn you and neither does your body. The CO detector is the cheapest device in the whole house that decides whether you wake up in time. It costs less than dinner and needs no installation, electricity or app.

The numbers are in official reports: according to the Toxicovigilance Program of the Ministry of Health, carbon monoxide is the most common toxic substance in Spanish emergency departments, with between 5,000 and 10,000 poisonings a year and an average of 125 deaths annually. And the National Plan for Preventive Actions against Low Temperatures 2025-2026 reminds us every winter that cold brings heating systems, braziers and generators: risk rises precisely when you shut yourself in the most.

1. What the standard requires: the table that decides whether it warns you in time

In Spain, everything is sold, from detectors at 15 € to devices at 80 €, and the difference is not the brand: it is whether they comply with the European standard UNE-EN 50291-1:2018 (ratified by AENOR in February 2021), which sets the test methods and performance requirements for domestic CO detectors. A compliant device must alarm within these times:

CO concentrationWhen the alarm must sound
30 ppmNot before 120 minutes (and must sound within that period)
50 ppmBetween 60 and 90 minutes
100 ppmBetween 10 and 40 minutes
300 ppmIn less than 3 minutes

Two readings. First: the standard does not monitor low background levels; rather, it warns before the damage is serious — that is why it does not go off with the chimney open one afternoon, but it does if the generator has been in the garage for half an hour. Second: once it goes off, the alarm does not quiet down until the level falls below 50 ppm: that it “switches itself off” does not mean the air is clean. For placement, EN 50292 applies (selection and installation): every room with a possible combustion source and every bedroom. The electrical side of the same risk is covered in passive fire safety and electrical fires.

If the budget is very tight, the priority is not the detector: it is ventilating and not heating with combustion inside the home. With that and a well-chosen flashlight you are already covered — the selection is in best stove without power and in maintaining temperature without electricity.

4. Where to put it (and where never to)

  • Yes: in every bedroom, in the hallway serving them and in the room where you burn anything (open kitchen, fireplace, workshop). Follow the manufacturer: most specify 1-3 meters from the source, on the ceiling or a high wall and away from drafts.
  • No: inside a cupboard, behind a curtain, above the extractor hood or next to a window: there the air arrives late or is renewed too quickly and it measures badly.
  • Garage: a domestic detector is not suitable for a running car: that requires higher-range models and, even better, not leaving the engine running in an enclosed space.
  • Maintenance: test monthly with the button, dust it every six months (recommended by the Comunidad de Madrid) and replace it at the end of its service life, 5 to 10 years depending on the model: the device itself will warn you.

5. Five mistakes that cancel out the detector

  • Trusting your sense of smell. CO has no smell at all: by the time you notice it, you have already been breathing it for a while.
  • Heating with the gas oven. The Ministry of Health says it literally: never use the stove or oven to heat a home.
  • Generator, chainsaw or boiler inside the home or garage. Combustion engines in an enclosed space are the classic source of mass poisoning.
  • Stove left on at night with the room closed. Ventilating is the cheapest measure: better to lose some heat than to have no air.
  • One detector for the whole house. Air does not travel between floors or through closed doors: at least one per floor and one in every bedroom.

6. If the alarm sounds: four steps

  1. Get everyone out of the room — without “checking what it is”, without turning anything off and without going back in to look for anyone.
  2. Ventilate from outside and do not go back until the emergency services authorize it.
  3. Call 112 from outside: people affected, symptoms and which combustion source was running.
  4. Have the source checked by a professional before turning it back on: a badly sealed boiler or chimney will repeat the episode.

And the warning most often ignored: headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion that ease when you leave the house are the typical picture. If you wake in the early hours with your head pounding and the heating on, get out.
