Dartnell's “The Knowledge”: what to read to rebuild everything after collapse

📅 2026-08-31 · ⏱ 7 min min read

There is a question that an emergency kit only half answers: what if things don't get fixed in 72 hours? What if you had to start from scratch? Because kits buy time, but knowledge is what turns that time into a new life.

What is «El Conocimiento»?

El Conocimiento: Cómo reconstruir nuestra civilización desde cero (The Knowledge: How to Rebuild Our World After an Apocalypse, 2015), by Lewis Dartnell — an astrobiology researcher at the University of Westminster — is a practical manual, not a doomsday essay. The premise is simple but powerful: you don't need to reinvent anything, just remember where the wheel is written down.

Dartnell starts from a realistic assumption: that society collapses tomorrow due to a pandemic, a prolonged power outage, a DANA like the one in Valencia in 2024, or a solar event (a G5-level CME like the Carrington Event of 1859). There are no elite survivors or underground bunkers: ordinary people, with limited access to tools, have to start over. The good news is that 99% of the necessary knowledge already exists in library books or old manuals.

And the best part: all the knowledge needed for the first 6 months (the most critical) is condensed into ~300 pages that you can read in a weekend.

The 7 blocks of essential knowledge

Dartnell organizes the manual into blocks that build on one another. They are not theory: they are practical recipes with local ingredients. These are the blocks that matter most in a Spanish prolonged emergency scenario:

1. Food — what you can't do without

The first problem after the collapse is not the lack of food, it is the lack of knowledge to produce it. Dartnell explains why growing wheat and legumes is the basis of all survival: wheat gives flour (bread), legumes give protein and fix nitrogen in the soil. The wheat-legume-crop rotation was the basis of Mediterranean agriculture for millennia, and still is.

The book details how to preserve seeds (select the best ears and store them in a dry place), how to make quick compost (organic waste + ash + water), and why composting at 60 °C eliminates pathogens and weed seeds. It also covers preservation methods without electricity: sun drying, salting, and preservation in fat (confit) that Spaniards already practiced in the Middle Ages.

If you are interested in starting today with practical products, the self-sufficient garden guide brings together the essential tools for growing in any space, and the 30-day kit expands your pantry beyond 72 hours.

2. Water — purifying without electricity

Water is the most critical resource: without it, the first week is already deadly. Dartnell describes three purification methods without electricity: boiling (5 min at a rolling boil, enough for 99.9% of pathogens), sand and charcoal filtration (a homemade filter with gravel, fine sand and charcoal reduces bacteria and improves taste), and chlorination with bleach (two drops of unscented bleach per liter, wait 30 min). The combination of filter + boiling gives reliable drinking water in any condition.

The European Union Preparedness Strategy (March 2025) recommends storing 3 liters per person per day just for drinking. For 72 hours, a family of 4 needs 36 liters. Water is renewed every 6 months and stored in opaque containers, away from sunlight.

3. Energy — from water to fire

Humanity's first technological leap was fire, but the first one that multiplies your work capacity is the water wheel. A basic water mill grinds grain, drives a bellows for the forge and pumps water. Dartnell details how to build it with local materials: wood, stone and ropes. A 2 m diameter wheel with a flow rate of 50 l/s produces enough energy for a rural house.

For the Spanish case: a stream with minimum flow during the rainy season (November-April in much of Spain) is enough to keep a mill running. In summer, battery backup power and solar generation take over. For those who need total autonomy in a blackout, the professional energy kit covers everything from 800 W of solar panels to SAI and Starlink.

4. Chemistry — soap, alcohol and acid

Soap is probably the most important chemical invention in history: hygiene is what has most reduced mortality since 1800. Dartnell explains the process of saponification: mixing fats (tallow, used oil) with caustic soda (sodium hydroxide) and water. The soda is obtained from wood ashes washed with water (leaching) and concentrated by boiling with ashes. With a homemade thermometer (boiling water = 100 °C) the reaction is controlled.

Ethyl alcohol is produced by fermenting sugars (the same process as beer or wine) and distillation. It serves as a disinfectant, fuel and preservative. Sulfuric acid is obtained by heating gypsum (calcium sulfate) to 900 °C, condensing the vapor, and diluting — with due precautions. From sulfuric acid, other essential compounds can be made: potassium nitrate for gunpowder, copper sulfate as a fungicide, and chlorine to purify water.

5. Medicine — prevent rather than cure

Without modern antibiotics, collapse medicine reduces to prevention and basic treatments. Dartnell emphasizes that most post-collapse deaths would be from infections, dehydration and hunger, not from wounds. The keys:

  • Hygiene: wash hands with soap, treat water, dispose of waste away from homes.
  • Natural antiseptics: honey (antibacterial), alcohol (disinfectant), chamomile tea (anti-inflammatory).
  • Basic anesthesia: DMT from coca leaves was historically studied, but in Spain the use of chamomile and rosemary as painkillers is tradition.
  • Fractures: immobilization with splints and bandages, elevation, ice (winter snow).

A well-assembled evacuation kit includes a tourniquet, gauze, bandages and antiseptic — things that cannot be made at home in 24 hours.

6. Materials — from clay to glass

Ceramics (clay + fire) are the basis of craftsmanship: pottery, bricks, tiles. Dartnell explains how to identify clay in the field (it feels greasy to the touch, can be shaped when wet) and fire pieces at 1000 °C (a field kiln with a chimney reaches the necessary temperatures). Glass is made from sand (silica), ash (sodium carbonate) and lime (calcium carbonate), melted at 1200 °C. In practice, glass is the hardest to produce — but ceramics are enough for most domestic needs.

7. Society and organization

Dartnell's last chapter is about the most forgotten topic: social organization. In a collapse, the individual does not survive: the community survives. The book proposes an organizational model based on «knowledge circles» — groups of 20-30 people where each member contributes a specialty (farmer, healer, blacksmith, builder). A community of 300-500 people (the size of a traditional Spanish village) is the minimum viable to maintain agriculture, crafts, defense and education.

Why this book now

Spain has had three years of extreme weather events: the DANA in Valencia (2024) left 200 dead and thousands affected; the 2023 heatwave was the most intense in 500 years; the fires in Galicia and Lisbon showed the vulnerability of rural areas. The Spanish Protección Civil has updated its emergency plans incorporating scenarios of «prolonged supply collapse» — the same scenario that Dartnell explores.

The difference between being prepared and being ready is knowledge. An emergency kit gives you water and food for 72 hours, but knowledge gives you the tools so that, when it runs out, you are not left in the dark.

Even with an intermediate level of English (B1), the book is fully accessible: Dartnell writes for the general public, not for academics. The recipes and processes are illustrated with clear diagrams that can be understood regardless of language.

If you read it, combine it with practice: a seed sprouter on the windowsill teaches more about survival agriculture than a hundred pages. Start small, start now. And always — knowledge is what no one can take away from you.
