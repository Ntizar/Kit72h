Dressing in layers: the technique that prevents deadly cold (2026)

📅 2026-09-09 · ⏱ 7 min min read

The Ministry of Health's Cold Weather Plan for the 2025-2026 season records that every winter low temperatures cause hundreds of avoidable deaths in Spain. They are not mountain accidents: they are older people in flats without sufficient heating, workers walking home in wet clothes, outings that run long when the temperature drops. The most repeated recommendation in official guides is always the same: dress in layers. Not for fashion — for physics.

Why layers and not one thick garment?

The human body loses heat mainly through the skin. Clothing does not generate heat: it traps it. And the best thermal insulator there is is still air. Three layers of thin fabric trap three air pockets; a single thick garment traps one. That is why Civil Protection and the Ministry of Health repeat word for word: “Bear in mind that several layers of thin clothing protect more than a single thick one, because they form insulating air pockets between them”.

Layers also let you adjust temperature. If you are walking and feel hot, take off a layer and do not sweat. If you sweat in a single thick garment and then stop, that wet clothing becomes a body cooler. Cotton cannot handle it: moisture takes you to hypothermia faster than dry cold.

The three layers: function and material

Layer 1: the base (next to the skin)

Its only job is to move sweat away from the skin. If the base gets wet, nothing you wear over it will save you. Valid options:

  • Merino wool: natural, absorbs moisture without losing heat, smells less than synthetic. Ideal for all-day wear. A 150-200 g merino base T-shirt costs between €25 and €50.
  • Polypropylene or technical polyester: synthetics move moisture away quickly and dry in hours. They are cheaper (€5-15) but can smell sooner and do not regulate temperature as well as wool.

Cotton is never used as a base layer. It absorbs sweat, holds it and stays stuck to the skin. When it gets wet — and it will get wet — it conducts cold like a sheet of water. In mountaineering slang, cotton “kills you”: it is literally the most dangerous material for cold emergencies. The Ministry of Health and the WHO agree: synthetic fibres and wool are the recommended options.

Layer 2: insulation (the most important)

This is where real heat is generated. The mid layer traps the air your body heats. Options:

  • Fleece: the king of value for money. Light, warm, dries fast. A mid-weight fleece costs €15-30 and works for everything: from a night without power to a Sunday walk.
  • Plika (PrimaLoft, Thinsulate, etc.): synthetic insulations that imitate down but work when damp. Ideal if there is a risk of rain. In rain jackets or insulated vests. Price: €20-60.
  • Down: the best insulator by weight, but if it gets wet it loses 90% of its capacity. Good for the kit at home, not for wearing if it rains or you sweat.

For the emergency kit, a fleece is the most versatile option: it works when damp, takes up little space, costs little and covers the widest temperature range.

Layer 3: the barrier (wind and rain)

Protects from the outside. Without this layer, wind strips the heat from the inner layers in minutes. It is called a “shell” in technical jargon.

  • Rain jacket or waterproof: with a waterproof membrane (IP ≥ 1500 mm) and sealed seams. A lightweight rain jacket (50-150 g) is so small it fits in any backpack. Price: €15-40 for basic functional models.
  • Rigid (hardshell): if prolonged heavy rain is expected (a DANA in winter, for example). More expensive (€40-80) but lasts for years.

The key is that the outer layer is breathable: if it is not, sweat inside condenses on the inside and you end up wet. Look for “breathable” or “breathable membrane”.

What does not get dressed properly: head, hands and feet

Ten percent of official sources forget it, but this is where frostbite and mild hypothermia begin:

  • Hat or scarf: heat is lost through the head. A wool or fleece hat costing €5-12 makes the difference between a mild night and constant shivering. A scarf protects the neck and stops wind getting under the coat.
  • Gloves: mittens are warmer than gloves (fingers share heat). A pair of wool or fleece gloves, €8-20.
  • Socks: two thin pairs work better than one thick pair (another air pocket). Merino wool or synthetic, never cotton. A pair of wool socks for emergencies costs €8-15.
  • Waterproof footwear: wet soles are the fastest entry point for cold. Waterproof boots or, at least, slip-resistant overshoes (€5-12) if you walk on snow or ice.

The system in practice: what to wear for each scenario

There is no single recipe for every temperature, but these combinations cover you from a Madrid winter to a snowfall in the Sistema Central:

Signs that something is wrong

The Ministry of Health and the Cold Weather Plan identify clear signs of hypothermia. If you detect them in yourself or another person, adding warm layers is the first step:

  • Uncontrollable shivering: the body tries to generate heat by fighting back. It is no longer uncomfortable; it is an emergency response.
  • Confusion or drowsiness: the brain loses function. The person may deny being cold.
  • Difficulty speaking: words become confused or impossible.
  • Clumsy hands: you cannot do up buttons or light a lighter.

If frostbite appears (skin white, yellowish, numb and firm) — do not rub the area and put it in warm (not hot) water. If you cannot walk on your feet, do not try to walk: you will make the injury worse.

Common mistakes

  • “A jacket is enough for me”: if it rains, if you sweat, if it is windy — it is not enough. A rain jacket on top is the difference between a night at home and a night in the emergency department.
  • Cotton as “I always wear it”: the denim shirt, the thick cotton sweater… they are normal daytime clothes. In an emergency, cotton is your enemy. Wool and synthetics keep you dry.
  • Not covering head and hands: you can have your torso covered and lose 40% of your heat through your head and extremities. A hat and gloves are not optional in extreme cold.
  • Storing warm clothing in the storage room: if the power goes out in January, you need warm clothing where you can grab it in 30 seconds, not where “you keep the winter stuff”.

What to include in the kit

For each family member, a bag with the basics to layer up:

  • Merino wool or synthetic base T-shirt
  • Mid-weight fleece
  • Lightweight rain jacket
  • Wool or fleece hat
  • Pair of gloves (wool or fleece)
  • Two pairs of wool socks
  • Scarf or thick neckerchief

This weighs less than 2 kg per person, takes up the space of a medium bag, and covers you from a January power cut to an isolated snowfall. If you live in northern or mountain areas, add a Plika (PrimaLoft) layer or a thermal vest.

A fact that is not scary: temperatures are rising

Winter 2025-2026 was, according to AEMET, the sixth warmest winter since 1961. Average temperatures were 1 °C above normal. That is not nothing: the last eight winters have been warmer than normal. But extreme cold waves do not disappear because of the average: when they come, they come suddenly (like the Arctic air episode that Civil Protection warned about in December 2026) and temperatures can drop 15-20 °C in hours. The layering system works for that sharp drop — the one almost nobody is prepared for.
