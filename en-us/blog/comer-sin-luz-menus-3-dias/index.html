What to eat when the power goes out: 3 days of no-cook menus (2026)

📅 2026-09-02 · ⏱ 5 min min read

28 April 2025 proved something that kit guides only half-admit: the problem with a long power outage isn't having food, it's having a full fridge turned into a countdown. Cooking without electricity can be improvised; managing what spoils cannot. Here are three days sorted: order of consumption, menus and the shopping that closes the loop.

The two rules that govern everything else

  • A closed fridge is your friend for the first 24-48 hours: a modern fridge, well stocked and kept closed, lasts a day and a half in the safe cool range; a full freezer, longer. Every opening takes time off: decide each morning what will be eaten and take it out in one go. And when you open it, keep your nose alert: anything that smells or looks odd goes in the bin. Food poisoning in the middle of a crisis is the last thing you need, and no amount of heroics is worth it.
  • No cooking doesn't mean no plan: easy-open canned goods, sliced bread, hard-skinned fruit, nuts, cereal bars, biscuits, vacuum-packed cured meat, cured cheese, chocolate. Fresh food that depends on cold is eaten in order: that is the core of this plan.

The order of consumption: first what spoils fastest

  • First 6-12 hours: whatever was already open or gets opened — yogurts, desserts, the plastic container from the fridge, bagged salad, the opened milk, the opened deli meat. If the outage is announced, this batch moves up to that night's dinner: it's time for a fridge party.
  • Day 1: sealed fresh food that needs cold — meat, fish, dairy close to its date — and only if you have somewhere to cook it: a camping stove, a gas burner, an outdoor barbecue (never indoors: carbon monoxide, the silent killer of every storm). Without a gas cooker, share this batch with neighbors who have one or accept the loss: you don't eat questionable fish out of pride.
  • Days 2 and 3 onward: pantry only — canned goods, canned pulses, bread, packaged flatbreads, hardy fruit, carton vegetable soup, powdered cocoa that can be mixed with water. The fridge is now a decorative cupboard: treat it as such.

Three days of menus (two adults, no electric hob or microwave)

Day 1 — Breakfast: sliced bread with peanut butter and fruit. Lunch: canned salad — canned chickpeas, tuna, tomatoes that have held up, a splash of oil — and fruit. Dinner: the fresh batch turned into a board of cured cheeses, vacuum-packed cured meat and breadsticks: the dinner that at home is normally called nibbles and here is called a plan.

Day 2 — Breakfast: packaged flatbreads or biscuits, an energy bar for anyone not hungry when they get up. Lunch: canned pulses dressed with tuna belly: served cold and nobody looks at it strangely. Dinner: carton vegetable soup or gazpacho, which kept at room temperature while sealed and passes as a hot dish without being one.

Day 3 — Breakfast: the last dairy products, which are at their cold-storage limit today anyway. Lunch: pasta you boiled extra on Day 1 on the camping stove, tossed with oil and canned goods; or sardines with bread if there wasn't enough water to boil. Dinner: back-of-the-cupboard — energy bars, nuts, chocolate and albacore with peppers: the classic camp dinner.

Notice the common thread across the three days: no meal depends on a single type of food. If the bread runs out, the canned food remains; and once a large can is opened, there is no fridge for the half that is left: you eat it all at that meal or you don't open it. In a power outage there are no leftovers: there are decisions.

The 40-60 euros of shopping that makes all this edible

  • Camping stove with two or three cartridges (15-20 € for the set): makes boiling pasta, rice or water normal whenever there is any doubt. And two spare butane cylinders: if the power grid is down, the home gas cooker works — the electric hob doesn't, the flame does.
  • Water for drinking and for rehydrating dehydrated food: always part of the budget.
  • Manual can opener: the most forgotten item in a food kit and the one that leaves the most cans unopened on the shelf.
  • No-wash tableware: a cup, plate and cutlery per person, because getting through the day is also about managing the water you won't use at the sink.
  • Heavy-duty trash bags: everything that spoils gets bagged and leaves the house quickly. A fridge rotting in the middle of July turns your kitchen into a problem that takes two weeks to resolve.

Mistakes that waste half your shopping

Storing your reserve next to bleach (the fumes contaminate even sealed packaging). Opening the large can thinking about tomorrow. Relying on smell as the only judge with milk that has been out for two days: when in doubt, throw it out, no debate. And discovering on day one that all the cans in the drawer need a can opener that is at your mother's house. These four mistakes, plus opening the fridge out of curiosity, explain 90% of the meals lost on 28-A.

The reserve for this scenario is put together in the power outage kit — stove, water, food that needs no refrigeration — and scaled up in the 30-day kit if you want a real pantry. The basics of food preservation are on the AESAN website.
