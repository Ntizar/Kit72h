How much water and food to keep at home: Switzerland's official quantities (and what Spain says)

There's a scene that repeats every time the power goes out in Spain. It's nine at night, your phone is dead, you open the tap and air comes out, and in the fridge there's two days' worth of food starting to warm up. Nobody told you how much water to have. Nobody gave you a list with numbers.

Switzerland does. Its Federal Office for National Economic Supply (BWL) has for years published an official domestic emergency reserve list, with exact quantities per person, and updated it in October 2025. It's not a mountain kit or an extreme survival list: it's what the Swiss state expects you to have in your cupboard at home so that a supply cut doesn't turn into an emergency.

In Spain, the public reference is the famous 72-hour kit. The concept is sound, but it comes without numbers: «water, non-perishable food, flashlight and radio». And without numbers, every home improvises. Here's the Swiss list translated, compared with what the Spanish guides say, and how to put it together in a real apartment.

The Swiss list, with its quantities

The Swiss official recommendation is more specific than we're used to. This is, literally, what they ask you to have at home at all times:

The most important nuance is in the Swiss text, not the table: the reserve is not designed for a long crisis. It's designed to bridge a temporary supply blockage. It's the difference between «I can't shop for a week» and «I have to survive a month in the mountains». That's exactly the same framework as the Spanish 72-hour kit, just with double the horizon and a quantified list.

What the Spanish guides say

In Spain there's no single figure published with Swiss clarity, but the Directorate-General for Civil Protection and Emergencies does recommend keeping a kit at home for the first 72 hours, and the regional self-protection guides specify the water: at least 1.5 liters per person per day just for drinking, and 3 liters per person per day if you also cook with that water.

That gives between 4.5 and 9 liters per person for three days. The 9 liters of the recommended Spanish minimum (drinking and cooking, three days) curiously match the 9 liters Switzerland asks for as a starting point. The difference isn't the water: it's the time.

Neither is wrong. The Spanish one is easier to take on and fits most real incidents (a storm, a DANA, a power cut of a few hours). The Swiss one is what happens when a country decides that domestic preparedness is public policy and writes it down with numbers.

Why water is always the first thing to fail

In Switzerland the drinking water supply is excellent, and yet the state insists on having its own reserve. The reason they give is concrete: a single storm is enough to break a pipe or contaminate the water. You don't need an apocalyptic scenario; you need roadworks, a flood or a pumping failure.

In Spain, moreover, there's a factor Switzerland doesn't have: heat. A person needs more water at 38 degrees than at 15, and in an August blackout the air conditioning doesn't exist. So if you copy the Swiss list, do it with 9 liters per person as a floor, not a ceiling: in an apartment without power in July, two people and a pet can easily need 25-30 liters for a week.

And a practical warning: bottled water expires. The reserve gets rotated, not stored forever. The BWL itself recommends integrating the food into your cooking routine — consuming and replenishing — and checking dates. If your jerrycan is two years old, it's not a reserve: it's a piece of furniture.

How to put it together in a Spanish apartment (with what you can buy today)

Here's the part no official list solves for you: what exactly to buy and how much it costs. This is the domestic version of the Swiss list, with ordinary products and reference prices in Spain.

1. Water: large bottles and a purifier

For two people, the Swiss base is 18 liters. In practice that's two 8-liter bottles per person, which stack in any cupboard and get rotated every six months. Add a purifier in case the water comes back cloudy: with tablets or a filter you turn doubtful water into drinkable water, and that's what makes the difference between 9 liters and a week.

2. Food for seven days, not three

The Swiss list is surprisingly normal: rice, pasta, oil, canned food, salt, sugar, coffee, UHT milk, dried fruit, chocolate. No astronaut rations. The rule is that it should be food you already eat, so you can rotate it without wasting money, and that at least part of it can be eaten without cooking. With canned food and dried fruit you cover the scenario of running out of gas.

5. What Switzerland added after the pandemic

The 50 masks per person is the figure that surprises most and best explains how an emergency list gets updated: it was added because a health crisis also leaves people without supplies. The four mistakes that keep repeating

  • Storing water and not rotating it. Bottled water expires and plastic bottles pick up a taste. Put a date on the cap and change it every six months.
  • Buying food you don't eat. If the emergency pantry is never touched, it all expires. The Swiss list works because it's normal food that goes into your kitchen rotation.
  • Forgetting medication. It's the most critical and the most personal point: a week of your chronic treatment, with prescription, in a cool place. No generic kit covers it.
  • Having nothing to heat things with. Water, rice and a stove: without the third, the first two only serve to go hungry in an orderly way.

What to copy from Switzerland and what not to

Copy the underlying idea: a closed list, with quantities and a review date. That's what turns preparedness into something verifiable instead of an intention. Also copy the water per person and the week of food if you have the space.

Don't copy the horizon as-is if you live in a small apartment: seven days of food for four people fills an entire pantry. Start with 72 hours done properly —water, food that doesn't need cold, radio, light, first-aid kit— and extend to a week in water and canned food, which is where space pays off most. If you want the ready-made version, the basic 72-hour kit covers the essentials and the blackout kit adds what you only need when there's no grid. And if you want the full Swiss list, with its quantities and its links, it's already put together in the 7-day Swiss kit.

What you shouldn't copy is the peace of mind of having it «noted in your head». A reserve that isn't counted doesn't exist: the day you need it you discover you have six bottles and half a bag of rice. Count the bottles tonight. It's the only step on this list that costs no money.
