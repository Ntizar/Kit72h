Returning home after a flood: mud, damp and insurance (2026)

📅 2026-09-02 · ⏱ 5 min min read

The days after a flood bring more scares than the flood itself: falls onto hidden debris, electric shocks in water that already seemed to have receded, smoke from damaged electrical installations, poisoning from generators put in the garage. Returning is a procedure, not an emotion. This is the order, step by step.

Before entering: the checks

  • You only return when official channels authorize it —112, Protección Civil, your local council—. The fact that the downstairs entrance hall is open is not authorization.
  • Inspect from outside: new cracks, a door that no longer closes as it used to, a sunken floor at the entrance, pillars. Any structural doubt: do not go in, call.
  • Electricity first: the main switch is turned off at the panel only if it is dry and accessible. If the panel is flooded or you are unsure, do not touch it: the utility company or an electrician should turn it off. An entrance hall with residual water conducts far better than your common sense believes.
  • Gas: if you smell it or the meter moves with everything turned off, get out and call the utility company or 112 from the street. No switches, no lighters, no turning on your mobile phone inside.
  • Minimum access equipment: tall boots, thick gloves, a mask against dry mud dust and decent light. The 'it's just wet house clothes' mindset does not work here.

The first 24 hours inside

  • Photos and videos before touching anything, inside and outside the house, in portrait so the walls are fully included: this is your evidence for the insurer, and from now on every object moved will be impossible to justify. If there is no signal, the photos can wait on your phone until you get out: documenting is worth more than saving the half-collapsed shelf first.
  • Floodwater is not water: it carries sewage, hydrocarbons and garage products. Anything it touched that cannot be recovered goes out; food that was in non-hermetic containers too, even if the cardboard seems dry. Personal hygiene items (toothbrushes, baby bottles, opened cosmetics) straight into the bin: it is one of the few rules without exception.
  • Mud: remove it, do not scrub it. First layers with a shovel, ideally when dry; scrubbing fresh mud only spreads an impossible film. And ventilation from minute one: cross-ventilation as soon as weather allows.
  • Wet appliances are not plugged in to see if they still work. They are dried, listed and assessed: the short circuit on being reunited with them is a story repeated in every DANA.
  • Mattresses, sofas, chipboard wardrobes, plasterboard: the non-recoverable items. They absorb dirty water and never dry out: mold guaranteed within 48 hours. The health of the house is worth more than the furniture: out the door.

Damp: the battle of the next three weeks

The house seems dry on Saturday and is soaking wet all month, and mold is the second disaster of every flood:

  • Constant ventilation: a permanently open window gap works better than opening for half an hour a day; and if you can rent or borrow dehumidifiers on the first day, they cut weeks off the ordeal. In homes with a basement, the basement is in charge: the air there does not move by itself.
  • Before replacing what was on the walls (paint, skirting boards, fitted furniture), check by touch —or with a cheap pin moisture meter— that the base material is truly dry. Trapping water behind a new wall is sowing the problem for next spring.
  • The most sensitive people (asthma, allergies, babies, older adults) are the first to pay for mold. If someone starts coughing or itching and the house is still damp, discuss with a healthcare professional whether not sleeping there yet is advisable: it is a health criterion, not a test of patience.

Insurance: it is won on day one, not in month one

  • Report it to the insurance company on the same day you return, with the date, cause —flood, the exact word matters— and a stable contact phone number if your line is down. Keep a written copy: the file lives where you wrote it.
  • Evidence is decisive: photos from now with a full walkthrough, a list of damaged belongings with model and approximate date, invoices wherever you have them (many are in your online shop's email: look for them calmly on a Sunday). And dig through your phone: Christmas photos in your living room prove water levels and the existence of belongings better than any account.
  • Emergency repairs: yes, but documented. Boarding up a broken window or cutting off a damaged line is your duty to prevent further damage and is usually covered: keep receipts and quotes for the loss adjuster, without waiting for them to arrive before leaving the house exposed.
  • If the home is rented, report the damage the same day and in writing to the landlord, with photos: who is responsible for what under the contract becomes much clearer with a date on the email.
  • And if your home falls within an area eligible for extraordinary public aid, ask at your local council about the registration and damage certificate that open the door: with every DANA, aid deadlines are missed because someone did not request a piece of paper in the first week.

The cheap lesson (and the most expensive one to learn)

Every DANA leaves homes whose ground floor floods for the second time six years apart. The lesson: in a flood zone, the important things live upstairs, and the electrical panel too if possible. Insurance is the patch, not the plan. Before signing a mortgage or lease, ask your local council or the river basin authority for the risk maps; and if you are already in one, adapt the DANA kit to your real history, not to whatever fear is in fashion.

The re-entry checks and access equipment are in the DANA/flood kit; the documentation you will need for insurance travels with the evacuation backpack and the originals are kept in the home kit.
