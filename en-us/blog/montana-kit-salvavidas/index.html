Emergency mountain kit: 15 items that really matter (2026)

📅 2026-08-31 · ⏱ 9 min min read

Many mountain rescues don't start with a technical climb: they start with a hiking route that runs long, fog that arrives earlier than forecast, a sprain that makes walking impossible, or a colder night than the forecast predicted. In 2024, the Guardia Civil's GREIM in Aragon carried out 609 interventions and assisted 942 people, of whom 462 were injured and 25 died, according to the Government Delegation in Aragon. Not all would have been avoided with a better backpack, but carrying the essentials and knowing how to use them reduces the margin for error.

The three that matter most when the weather worsens

Hypothermia isn't only a snow or high-mountain problem. The CDC notes that it can occur at relatively mild temperatures if clothing is wet and there is wind. Heat loss increases with wet clothing and wind exposure; wind chill isn't a universal figure for heat lost, but a way to express how cold you feel. These three items cover the basics:

  • Emergency thermal blanket: it doesn't generate heat. What it does is reflect some of your body heat and serve as a barrier against wind and rain if used wrapped around you and dry. It weighs very little (light models are around 30-60 g) and takes up less space than a phone. It doesn't replace dry clothing or a tent, but it can help while rescue arrives. See thermal blankets on Amazon ↗
  • Rain jacket or waterproof poncho: a windproof and waterproof layer keeps clothing from getting wet and prevents wind from cooling your skin. An emergency poncho weighs 50-150 g depending on the model. It isn't the same as a technical jacket, but it fits in any backpack and can make a difference during a wait. See emergency ponchos on Amazon ↗
  • LED headlamp with spare batteries: running out of light turns a two-hour walk into a whole night. A headlamp leaves your hands free to walk, eat, or signal. Runtime depends on the model and power mode: carry spare batteries and test the headlamp before you set out. See LED headlamps on Amazon ↗

The mountain first-aid kit: it's not the one from home

A first-aid kit doesn't replace training. If you go out often, take a basic first-aid course and learn how to respond to bleeding, a sprain, and an allergic reaction. For day-to-day use, a light first-aid kit should cover:

  • Waterproof adhesive bandages, sterile gauze, and medical tape: cleaning and covering a wound prevents friction and dirt from making it worse. Also carry nitrile gloves.
  • Elastic bandage and compression bandage: for an ankle or knee sprain, temporary immobilization helps you retreat. Don't tighten until you cut off circulation; if the fingers turn cold or purple, loosen it.
  • Sunscreen and SPF lip balm: ultraviolet radiation increases with altitude, and snow reflects some of it. Use a high factor and reapply; a burn isn't noticeable until it's already done.
  • Personal medication, only as prescribed: if you have severe allergies, asthma, or diabetes, carry your medication and treatment plan. An antihistamine doesn't treat anaphylaxis; if your doctor has prescribed auto-injectable adrenaline, carry it and learn how to use it. Don't improvise doses or carry medications you don't know how to use.

For cuts that bleed heavily, the first measure is direct pressure with clean gauze. A tourniquet should only be used by someone who knows how to do it and when bleeding isn't controlled; don't improvise one for a minor wound. The basic 72h kit guide has the home first-aid kit; this is the one that goes with you in your backpack.

Communication: a mobile phone isn't always enough

In valleys, gorges, and mountain areas, coverage can disappear. A mobile phone is still the first tool for calling 112, but you shouldn't rely on it alone. Before setting out, check that you have battery, an offline map app, and the emergency number at hand. If there's no coverage, your exact location and message may have to wait.

  • Emergency whistle: the international mountain distress signal is six signals per minute (whistles or flashes), with a one-minute pause, then repeat; it is not three whistles, it is six. It isn't the only signal and doesn't replace 112, but it carries farther than your voice and uses no energy. See emergency whistles on Amazon ↗
  • 5,000-10,000 mAh power bank: keeps your phone on a little longer, but the number of recharges depends on the phone, the cold, and usage. Carry it charged and with a cable; don't leave it at the bottom of your backpack. See power banks on Amazon ↗
  • Battery-powered or hand-crank FM radio: it can receive stations if there is radio coverage, but it doesn't guarantee receiving a specific alert. In Spain, emergency alerts also arrive via the ES-Alert system on mobile phones. Radio is a plan B, not the only source of information. See emergency radios on Amazon ↗

Navigation: map, compass, and a plan someone else knows about

Fog and darkness erase landmarks that seemed obvious. A GPS can fail or run out of battery, so navigation can't depend on a single screen.

  • Topographic map of the area and compass: learn to read them before you set out. IGN publishes the MTN25 series (1:25,000) for the whole country, and specialist publishers produce hiking maps for specific areas. A map lets you identify trails, shelters, and elevations, but it's useless if you don't know how to use it. See topographic maps on Amazon ↗
  • Offline map on your phone: download the area before you set out and save a screenshot of the route. Don't count on having coverage at the point where you need it most.
  • Backup light and batteries: a second, independent light source keeps you from being left in the dark if your headlamp fails. Don't mark the trail with plastic or cord: besides being unreliable, it leaves waste in the mountains.

Water, food, and shelter: what you can't improvise

There's no universal amount of water for a route: it depends on temperature, humidity, effort, altitude, and whether there are water sources. The European reference (EFSA) is 2.0 liters per day of total water for adult women and 2.5 liters for men, including water from food and other drinks, under moderate temperature and activity conditions; with exertion and heat, needs increase. Carry more than you think you need and plan refill points.

  • Extra water: at least one more bottle than planned for the route. If it's hot or there's a lot of elevation gain, increase the margin. See mountain bottles on Amazon ↗
  • Purification: a portable filter or purification tablets let you treat water from springs, but you must follow the manufacturer's instructions and not trust a stream based on how it looks. If in doubt, boil the water or don't drink it. See purifiers on Amazon ↗
  • Emergency food: 300-400 extra kcal in the form of nuts, bars, or chocolate are a buffer if the day runs long. It isn't a survival ration: it's energy to keep walking and make better decisions.
  • Rain jacket and warm layer: even if you set out in sunshine. In the mountains the weather changes fast, and a waterproof layer can keep the cold from stopping you before fatigue does.

Tools and repair

  • Multi-tool knife: useful for cutting tape, opening a package, or tightening a screw. Before buying one, check your region's regulations on blade size and locking; don't carry it as a weapon. See multi-tool knives on Amazon ↗
  • Cloth adhesive tape: wrapped around a pen, it takes up almost no space and can repair a sole, a trekking pole, or a tarp. It isn't a substitute for a proper repair, but it gets you out of a jam.
  • 3-5 meter utility cord: for tying a tarp, securing a backpack, or improvising shelter. It isn't climbing gear and isn't suitable for belaying a person: that requires certified rope and training. See utility cord on Amazon ↗

Before you set out: the plan that saves more than gear

Tell someone you trust your route, who you're going with, and what time you expect to be back. If you don't arrive at the expected time, that person should know who to call. In Spain, the emergency number is 112. Don't wait until dark to ask for help if you're lost or someone can't walk.

Check AEMET's mountain forecast, not just the valley forecast: the website distinguishes mountain areas and provides specific information on summits, wind, temperatures, and snow. Mountain forecast — AEMET ↗

And remember: the summit will always be there. Coming back another weekend is free; no summit is worth a life.
