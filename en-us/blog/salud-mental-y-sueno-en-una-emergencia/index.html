Mental health and sleep in an emergency: how to keep going (2026)

📅 2026-09-24 · ⏱ 12 min min read

It's 21:40 and the power goes out. Twenty minutes later you've sorted the fridge: you know what to throw away and what not to. What no one has taught you to sort out is the other part. The child asking whether the power will come back before bedtime, your mother calling you at two in the morning frightened because she can't see anything, or your own brain at three still going over the list of things that could still go wrong. Emergencies aren't won with flashlights and water bottles alone: they're won by sleeping, eating and keeping your head in the right place. This is the part of the kit that doesn't go in a box.

And this is not a soft or secondary issue. Sustained stress takes away your appetite and your sleep, and when you've had two nights with almost no sleep you make worse decisions: you leave the door open, you drive at night to 'get there sooner', you don't see the puddle across the road. WHO has studied this for years in its emergency guidance, and the conclusion is very practical: what helps in the first 72 hours is not a speech, it's four concrete things —safety, information, human contact and rest— and all of them can be prepared today.

1. What official manuals say: look, listen, connect

The reference guide in Spanish is “Psychological first aid: Guide for field workers”, by WHO with War Trauma Foundation and World Vision International. It is intended for people helping others in a disaster, but the framework is so simple that it works just as well for a neighbour, for your father or for yourself. It comes down to three actions.

  • Look: check that you are in a safe place, who needs help first and what basics are missing: water, warmth, a dry place, real information. Before talking about feelings, you deal with thirst, cold and noise.
  • Listen: approach, introduce yourself, ask what they need at that moment and don't force them to tell their story. The guide itself recalls a conclusion from the WHO mhGAP working group: a very distressed person should be offered psychological first aid, not forced to go over what happened. Let those who want to talk talk, yes; interrogate those who don't, no.
  • Connect: help them reach family, services that are working and practical information. One good piece of information given in time (when the power will come back, where there is drinking water, which road is closed) calms more than any nice phrase.

What you can do in the next 10 minutes: drink a glass of water and eat something even if you're not hungry. Sit down and do two minutes of slow breathing (four seconds in, six seconds out). Use the 5-4-3-2-1 technique —name in a low voice five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can touch, two you can smell, one you can taste— when you notice your head going in loops. And tidy one single room to turn it into your 'command post': a table with the radio or charged phone, the flashlight, water, paper and pen. Visible order translates into calm.

What seems like a good idea but isn't: using alcohol to sleep (it leaves you asleep for the first part of the night and awake and more anxious for the second), having the TV or radio with news on a loop at all hours (it feeds the sense of catastrophe and doesn't give you a single useful fact), borrowing a sleeping pill from a neighbour (if you use something to sleep, do it through your doctor or pharmacist) and saying 'we'll sleep when this is over', because sleep debt is also paid in the next day's decisions.

2. Sleeping when there is no light, no silence and no calm

The second or third night of an emergency is the most critical moment: you have used up the adrenaline of the first day and you arrive shattered, but your head won't slow down. Four measures solve much of the problem, and none costs money if you already have the kit put together.

  • Fixed times. Go to bed and get up at the same time, even if you are not working and sleep in fits and starts. The internal clock doesn't understand blackouts: it understands routine.
  • The bed is for sleeping only. If you haven't fallen asleep after 20-30 minutes, get out of bed and do something calm in dim light —read, write, tidy— until sleep returns. It is the classic recommendation of sleep guides and works better than tossing and turning for rebound insomnia.
  • Less caffeine in the afternoon and no stimulants in the middle of the night. Coffee, tea and cola drinks stay in your system for hours; if you're on watch, ration them.
  • Cover screens and use a battery-powered clock. The phone informs you and steals your sleep: set two times a day to check the news —in the morning and late in the afternoon— and leave the phone charging in another room at night. A battery-powered alarm clock for €8-12 saves you from looking at the time at 3:40 in the morning.

For the environment, three common-sense things almost nobody prepares: darkness (a sleep mask, or an improvised curtain, because a streetlight or an LED can leave your face lit all night), silence (foam earplugs; if the noise is outside, a radio station talking softly or a constant radio sound masks it better than nothing) and coolness or warmth depending on the season —if the problem is cold or heat, you have the full rules in how to maintain body temperature without electricity. And a golden rule: never sleep on bare floor; mat, cardboard and blanket in layers, as explained in dressing in layers.

If something at home needs watching —a sick person, the water level, the downstairs neighbour— don't let the same person always do it. Divide the night into blocks and write it on paper, not from memory:

BlockWho is on watchWho sleeps
22:00 - 01:00Person APerson B
01:00 - 04:00Person BPerson A
04:00 - 07:00Person APerson B (or a 30-min nap)

The watch shift is deliberately short: three hours can be sustained, six cannot. And one last tool that almost always works: the notebook by the bed. Before you go to bed, write down the three or four worries that are going to wake you and what you can do about each one tomorrow. Getting them out of the mental loop and leaving them on paper is the cheapest way for the brain to accept letting them go.

3. Children: routine calms more than any explanation

A child doesn't need to know the full truth of a disaster: they need to know what is going to happen in their day. The presence of an adult who doesn't shout and routine —water, teeth, story, light— do more than any conversation. Three rules for tonight:

  • Tell them the truth, but keep it short and give them a plan. “There's no power because there's a very big outage. We're at home, there's water and food, and Mum and Dad are here. Tomorrow we'll look at it with the flashlight.” No details that will scare them and no promises you can't keep (“tomorrow the power will definitely come back” is a promise; “tomorrow I'll tell you as soon as they know” is not).
  • Give them a job. What most reduces children's fear is having a role: carrying the flashlight, noting the times, checking the water containers, handing out the blankets. It is their way of feeling they control something.
  • Sleep next to them if they ask. This is not the night to teach them to sleep alone. The comfort object (blanket, soft toy, pacifier, story) comes out of the cupboard and goes into the evacuation bag from now on.

Also: no news on a loop in front of them, and a dim light in the hallway all night so they don't wake up in total darkness they don't recognise. If after several days the child doesn't eat, doesn't sleep or is very irritable, talk to your paediatrician. You have the detail by age in babies and children in an emergency and the materials in the baby and breastfeeding kit.

4. Older people, people with chronic illness and those who live alone

Here preparation happens before the disaster, not during it. Darkness disorients older people faster than hunger: they lose their sense of the hallway, get up at night and fall. Concrete solutions:

  • Beacon lights in the hallway and bathroom. Four LED battery lights that come on by themselves cost less than €15 and prevent the most common fall during a blackout. Everything an older person's kit needs is detailed on its page.
  • Towel at the edge of the bed and a clear path. No chairs or boxes between the bed and the door: at night and without light, they become an obstacle.
  • A call at the same time every day. It's not a chat: it's a welfare check. And always with a designated backup person, because the person making the call can get sick too. It is the basis of what I covered in living alone and being prepared.
  • Check in in person, not by WhatsApp. If there is hearing loss, a message is no use: you have to knock on the door, speaking loudly and facing them. The same applies to mobile alerts, which should be explained beforehand.

With chronic medication, the order of the day is to keep to the schedule and not improvise doses: pill organiser and a written list, with the days marked. If there are insulins or medicines that need to be kept cold, check how to ensure 2-4 weeks without a pharmacy and always consult your doctor or pharmacist for any decision about doses or storage. And if the person has cognitive impairment, don't argue with their reality: redirect —water, blanket, hand, song, photo—.

5. The person holding things together can also fall apart

In every home there is someone who acts as the switchboard: they call, organise, don't sleep, and after three days they burn out. If you are that person, this is for you.

  • No one stays on watch two nights in a row. Write it on the fridge door in large letters, because it's the first thing everyone skips.
  • Eat and drink even if you're not hungry. Five minutes of salty food and a glass of water every three hours do more than a coffee on the run.
  • Name a backup out loud. “If I can't, call Marta and she knows where everything is.” Above all, write the important phone numbers down on paper: the full list is in the official resources you should have saved.
  • Get outside for five minutes a day. Fresh air, the street, walking. WHO has documented it: physical activity reduces symptoms of anxiety and depression and improves sleep, and that counts double when everything is upside down.

And set an honest limit on your role: you don't have to fix anyone. Accompanying, sorting out the practical things and handing over the baton is already all that can be done on the first day. If you or someone in your home has gone several days without sleeping or eating, if sadness settles in or ideas of not wanting to live appear, that is not managed with willpower: call 024, the suicidal behaviour helpline of the Ministry of Health, free and available 24 hours a day, or 112 if the emergency is immediate. With any clinical doubt, consult your doctor or pharmacist.

And this is the seven-day plan to have all this sorted before you need it, at fifteen minutes a day:

  • Day 1. Write on a sheet of paper the key phone numbers (112, 024, doctor, school, two neighbours, two family members) and the family meeting point. One paper copy in your wallet and another taped inside the hall cupboard.
  • Day 2. Set up the command table: radio, flashlight, charger, water, paper, pen. One table, always the same one.
  • Day 3. Buy earplugs, sleep mask and battery-powered clock. They go in the bedside drawer, not the storage room.
  • Day 4. Prepare the 'dinner without power': cans, nuts, fruit and water accessible, and a tray with what you need (can opener, spoon, napkins) so you don't open cupboards in the dark.
  • Day 5. Try a two-hour night without mains power at home: flashlights, safely placed candles, no screens. You'll discover what's missing before you need it.
  • Day 6. Explain the plan to the children and give them a specific task (flashlight, water list). Ten minutes is enough.
  • Day 7. Call the family member who lives alone and agree the time of the daily call. And tell a neighbour who to contact if they don't see you.

With this you have covered what no flashlight can fix: sleeping, eating, keeping your head together and knowing who to call. If you want to divide the work by scenario, start by putting together your 72-hour kit and the first decisions of a blackout; and if tonight you are already in the middle of the mess, the urgent stuff is in the home or shelter-in-place kit.
