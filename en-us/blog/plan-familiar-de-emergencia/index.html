Family emergency plan: meeting point and drill (2026)

📅 2026-09-02 · ⏱ 5 min min read

Every real emergency reveals the same thing: those who had a family plan move, and those who didn't improvise with their hearts racing. A plan isn't a document: it's four decisions made in advance and known to everyone, including the people who don't live with you every day (grandma, the father who does Friday pickup). It takes an afternoon to build and a review every six months to keep up.

1. The meeting point, on two levels

  • Neighborhood level (immediate): a specific, visible place within walking distance for an evacuation of the building or street: the fountain in the square, the gas station on the way out, the bar on the corner. Don't say the square: say the fountain. In chaos, vague meeting points separate people more than they bring them together.
  • City level (if the first one no longer exists): a second point outside your area for when the neighborhood gets cut off: a relative's home at another address, a station, a place everyone has heard of. And a simple rule: if no one reaches point 1 within an hour, we all go to point 2.
  • The school has its own protocol for handing children over to families: ask where they would hand them over before the day you need it, and that point goes on your map. It's the detail no one remembers to ask for.

2. The beacon: your out-of-area contact

When the network is overloaded, local calls get stuck and a twenty-character SMS gets through where a conversation won't. Hence the classic civil protection technique: designate someone who lives far away —another region, ideally another country— as the message hub. Everyone writes to the beacon where they are and how they are; the beacon sends back the full picture: your sister is okay at a friend's house, your father isn't answering his phone but sent an SMS at 9:12. One person coordinating from outside is worth ten improvising inside. And the beacon must know how to be a beacon: takes notes and doesn't judge, and doesn't raise the alarm in the first hours of silence, because every emergency has normal silences.

3. Roles: who acts when no one is making good decisions

In a scare, you don't think: you execute what you've practiced. Assign the four typical tasks, with a named person and a backup (if the assigned person isn't home, the role can't disappear with them):

  • Children and pets: the person who physically picks them up and gets them out. With a baby at home, two people: one carries the child, the other carries the child's bag.
  • Utilities and door: the person who shuts off the gas if it smells, turns off the water if there's flooding, disconnects what can be reached and locks up on the way out. If there's no time, you leave: no house is worth half a minute inside with the alert sounding.
  • Kit and documents: the person who grabs the backpack and the folder, both on the way out, never in the storage room at the back.
  • Information: the person who turns on the radio and checks the official warning (112, civil protection), with the house rule written down: no messaging rumor is shared within the family without an official source. That rule prevents more disasters than you imagine.

4. The three rules that replace all the communication you won't have

The plan has to work without network and without battery, on paper:

  • Silence rule: if there's no contact within X hours, we all assume we're at the meeting point (or at the beacon's home if the neighborhood is cut off), and no one searches for anyone across a city in chaos. Searching on your own is a different risk, and often greater than waiting where the plan says.
  • School rule: if you can't come for the children, they stay with the teachers and the school's protocol takes over. The children know it by heart and the adults don't betray it by going to pick them up on their own: disrupting a school's dismissal puts the whole group at risk, including yours.
  • Return rule: you go home when the official channels say so, not when the guy in the building across the street says it's okay now. Every DANA has its story of a premature return told in the garage.

The drill: a Tuesday night, twenty minutes

An undrilled plan is just a piece of paper on the fridge. Make the review part of your seasonal wardrobe change, twice a year:

  • Everyone says their part from memory: my point, my role, the beacon's number. Whoever gets it wrong listens; no scolding, just repeat it.
  • Timed evacuation drill: backpacks, folder, shoes on, out on the street in under ten minutes. That's where the failures no document reveals come out: the key that wasn't where you thought, the zipper that won't close with a coat caught in it, the child who can't find their shoes in the dark.
  • Equipment check: expiry dates, batteries, the small amount of cash in the folder, that the children's kit headlamp turns on, that the ID card is still inside the older child's jacket.
  • Updating what changes: grandparents' new contacts, trusted neighbors who moved, school routines. The plan ages because the family changes.

And a trick that works: during drills, the children hold the stopwatch and keep score. What at seven is a game with scores is still in their heads at twelve on the day it really goes off.
