Emergency food: what to eat, how much to store, and how to rotate it without throwing anything away

📅 2026-10-02 · ⏱ 9 min min read

Why food expires before the emergency

Water shortage is the priority, and you should not wait until you are intensely thirsty to look for it. Food is the second thing people forget when preparing a kit, and also the thing that spoils most often: people buy cans or special "emergency" tins that end up in the trash because they expire. The solution is simpler: the best emergency food is the food you already eat.

Many European and Spanish guides recommend a reserve of at least 72 hours; it is not a legal obligation. The key is not to buy "emergency food" but to rotate what you already eat: that way the reserve is used, replenished, and does not become a museum of cans.

The golden rule: buy to eat, not to store

The most effective method is FIFO (First In, First Out — first in, first out). It works like this:

  1. Buy an extra of what you already consume regularly: one more pack of pasta, two more cans of tuna, an extra packet of crackers.
  2. When you use the extra in your normal life, put it straight back into the reserve.
  3. Your emergency stock rotates by itself, without you having to think about dates or expiry dates.

This turns an "emergency reserve" into something that requires no management: it is simply your usual pantry with an extra 20-30%. If you already do your shopping every week, you do not need a special purchase: add one unit of what you already eat and let rotation do the rest.

There is no single figure: it depends on whether you have a kitchen available or not. If the blackout leaves the fridge not working but you can heat canned food over a fire, you need fewer ready meals. If you have no safe way to cook, you need everything ready to eat.

The "double packet" rule in practice

The simplest method that works does not require inventories or dates. Every time you go to the supermarket, apply these rules:

  • Canned foods: if you buy a pack of 4 cans of tuna, add 2 more. The extra ones are your reserve; do not separate them from the pantry.
  • Pasta or rice: if you use a 500 g package a week, keep another one closed. One is consumed, the other restocks.
  • Crackers or biscuits: an extra package. They are the first-day snack, when you still do not want to open a can.
  • Nuts: an extra bag. They expire before canned foods, so they go first in the rotation.

With normal shopping and a little foresight, a family can have the complete reserve without a huge outlay. You do not need to fill a cupboard all at once: rotation does the work.

When to check the reserve: a practical schedule

You do not need a calendar or alarms. Do the check every time the clocks change (March and October), just as you would with remote-control batteries:

  • Check dates: 2 minutes. Look at the dates on nuts, crackers, powdered milk, and canned foods.
  • Complete what is missing: if something has expired, replace it with a new one and put it at the front.
  • Replenish what has been used: if the reserve has gone down (because of a trip or outing), complete it with your next shop.
  • Verify special needs: if there are babies, allergens, or restricted diets, check that the stock is still valid and sufficient.

Preparedness is a habit, not a one-off act. Checking twice a year takes less than 10 minutes and prevents throwing food away or discovering a problem when there is no supermarket left.

Common mistakes with emergency food

1. Buying special "emergency food." Freeze-dried meals can last for years, but they also cost more and do not always match what you eat. With canned foods and dry foods you already consume, you have a reserve that rotates and is not wasted.

2. Having nothing ready to eat. If the power goes out and you do not have a safe cooking setup, unopened cans will not solve dinner. Keep bars, crackers, and nuts for the first day.

3. Storing food in a hot or humid place. Heat and humidity degrade packaging and food. Choose a cool, dry, dark place; if the pantry gets very hot in summer, find another spot.

4. Forgetting salt and hydration. Salt is useful for cooking, but it is not a medicine and does not by itself prevent fainting. In an emergency, water and medical care come first.

5. Not thinking about dependents. Babies (formula or infant milk), older people (soft or special diets), pets, and people with allergies have their own needs. Each one needs their own reserve and check.

Do canned foods really expire?

For canned foods, the date usually indicates quality rather than safety: an intact can with no dents at the seams, no rust, no bulging, and no leaks may keep its quality beyond the "best before" date, but that does not authorize adding years to the date. If in doubt, discard it. But do not use that as a general rule: if the can is dented, bulging, smells bad, or releases gas when opened, discard it. When in doubt, do not risk it.

Can you cook without electricity or gas?

Yes, but you need a safe heat source and ventilation. A camping stove should only be used outdoors or in a well-ventilated space; never inside a home, a tent, or a car, because of the risk of carbon monoxide. Without a safe cooking setup, eat ready-to-eat foods and do not improvise with fuels.

What should you do if there are babies or allergens?

For babies, always follow the label on formula or infant milk and the preparation instructions; do not use expired products and discard any leftover after a feeding. If there are allergies, keep foods labeled as free from the allergen and check the labels every time: formulations change. For medical diets, consult a professional before setting up the reserve.
