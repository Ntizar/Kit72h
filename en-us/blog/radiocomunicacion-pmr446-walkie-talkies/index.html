PMR446 two-way radio: legal, license-free walkie-talkies (2026)

📅 2026-09-06 · ⏱ 7 min min read

28 April 2025 showed what preparedness guides have been repeating for years: when all mobile networks collapse at once, you are left mute. WhatsApp doesn't work, calls don't drop — they simply don't exist. And during a DANA, cell towers can be without power for days. This is where PMR446 radio communication comes in, and why more and more families are including it in their emergency kit.

What is PMR446?

PMR446 (Personal Mobile Radio 446) is a personal-use radio communication service operating in the 446 MHz UHF band (specifically 446.0 to 446.2 MHz). It has been regulated at European level by CEPT (European Conference of Postal and Telecommunications Administrations) since 1998 and is fully legal in Spain without a license. There is nothing to process, no fee to pay, no exam to take. Anyone can buy a PMR446 device and use it.

The rules are simple:

  • Maximum power: 0.5 watts (500 mW) — enough for 2-5 km in the city and up to 10 km in open country.
  • Fixed, built-in antenna — you are not allowed to replace it with a larger antenna (that would require a license).
  • 16 channels in the standard band — numbered 1 to 16, plus 8 additional channels if the device supports the digital standard (ECC Decision 05/12).
  • 180-second timeout per transmission — prevents someone from blocking the channel without realizing it.

The key is that it does not depend on any infrastructure: no towers, no carrier, no coverage. Devices communicate directly with each other (simplex mode). If you and your neighbor have PMR446 walkie-talkies, you can hear each other even if the entire network goes down.

Why is it relevant in an emergency?

PMR446 radio communication is the only means of communication between people that requires no infrastructure at all. It does not depend on towers (like a mobile phone), does not depend on the internet (like WhatsApp), and does not depend on electricity (if it runs on batteries or a rechargeable battery). That makes it the missing link between FM radio for listening (receive-only) and the mobile phone (which needs a network).

Protección Civil already knows this: the National Emergency Radio Network (REMER) has relied on amateur radio operators with radio equipment since the 1960s, and the Ministry of the Interior has published specific material on operating emergency communications equipment. In July 2026, the Government went even further: it enabled additional PMR frequencies for regional emergency services, authorizing their use automatically without prior formalities when civil protection teams move between regions. Radio communication is not a hobby: it is critical infrastructure.

Channels and sub-tones: the emergency configuration

PMR446 radios have 16 main channels (some models have 32 with the digital standard). Each channel can be configured with a CTCSS sub-tone (privacy code) to filter interference. For emergencies, the recommended configuration is:

  • Channel 7, sub-tone 7: this is the commonly used PMR446 channel for emergencies. Anyone on this frequency can try to make contact without congesting other channels. Use it for the emergency call and for the meeting point.
  • Channel 1, sub-tone 1: family or group communication channel. If your family has 4 walkie-talkies, assign them Channel 1 with sub-tone 1 so you do not mix with other users.
  • Channel 16, no sub-tone: open channel for listening. Useful for monitoring whether someone is talking before you transmit.

And here is the most important communication rule: always check that no one is talking before you press the button. Simultaneous transmissions produce an unintelligible squeal and block the channel. If they don't understand you, repeat. If there is noise, wait 10 seconds and try again.

Real range: what the box says vs. what happens

The box says "10 km" or "20 km". That is with perfect line of sight, at sea, with nothing between the two devices. In reality:

  • In the city (buildings, narrow streets): 0.5-2 km. Concrete walls attenuate the signal a lot.
  • In an open neighborhood or residential development: 2-4 km.
  • In open country: 5-10 km.
  • In the mountains (peak to peak): up to 15-20 km.

The practical rule: height is everything. If you are on a high floor and your neighbor is on another, you can hear each other at 3 km. If you are both in the valley, it already gets difficult at 500 meters. To improve: go up to the terrace or the highest balcony, and point the walkie-talkie vertically (the antenna is omnidirectional but works better vertically).

Typical mistakes

  • Buying walkie-talkies that are not PMR446: many cheap walkie-talkies on Amazon operate on FM or CB bands that require a license. Look explicitly for "PMR446" in the description. If it doesn't say so, don't buy it.
  • Letting the batteries run down: check the batteries every 6 months (the same reminder as for the water in the kit). If they are rechargeable, keep them charged with a power bank or solar charger.
  • Not practicing: a walkie-talkie that is never used is an expensive brick. Test it once a month with a neighbor or family member. You will see whether it works, learn to use it, and know where coverage drops in your area.
  • Listening to what is not yours: on the open channel (Channel 16 with no sub-tone) you will hear other people's conversations. Do not intervene, do not repeat what you hear. You have the right to listen, but not the right to transmit on a frequency that is not yours.
  • Confusing PMR446 with amateur radio: amateur radio requires an exam and a license (HF/VHF/UHF, 10-100 W power, regional/national range). PMR446 does not: it is plug-and-play, with no exam. They are different things.

Integration with your emergency kit

PMR446 walkie-talkies perfectly complement what you already have in the basic kit:

  • FM radio (listen) + PMR446 (talk) = complete two-way information coverage.
  • Spare batteries for the walkie-talkie in the same bag as those for the radio and headlamp.
  • Power bank to charge rechargeable walkie-talkies: a 10,000 mAh power bank charges a PMR446 walkie-talkie about 10 times.
  • Name labels: stick an adhesive label with each user's name on each walkie-talkie. During a night evacuation, knowing whose device is whose is a detail that prevents confusion.

Radio as a habit, not as a purchase

A PMR446 walkie-talkie bought and stored in a cupboard is useless. What works is the habit of using it: test one with your partner, with a neighbor, with your hiking group. You will see that they fall short in the city, that in the mountains they are another matter, and that Channel 7 with sub-tone 7 is the one that will save you if everything else fails. It is a cheap (15-40 € per pair) and legal accessory that, at the right moment, is the only means you have to say "I'm fine" to the person who matters most.
