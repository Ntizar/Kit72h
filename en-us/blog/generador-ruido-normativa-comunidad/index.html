Generator and noise in the community: how many dB will they tolerate (2026)

📅 2026-10-14 · ⏱ 6 min min read

A two-day blackout, the generator you bought 'just in case' and, at 11:40 p.m., a knock at the door: the neighbour can't sleep. The answer to 'and is this legal?' isn't in any forum: it's in the Law 37/2003 on Noise, your council's ordinance and the Horizontal Property Act.

1. National law: where domestic activity begins and where it stops being domestic

The Law 37/2003 of 17 November on Noise is the main rule, and Article 2.2.a) provides the key: excluded from its scope are 'domestic activities or the behaviour of neighbours, when the noise pollution produced by them remains within tolerable limits in accordance with municipal ordinances and local customs'.

Translation: switching on your generator is a domestic activity… until it stops being one. As soon as you exceed what your council considers tolerable, you enter the penalty regime: Article 28.5 allows local ordinances to classify as an infringement 'noise produced by domestic activities or by neighbours'.

2. The figures in Royal Decree 1367/2007: how many dB are tolerated at night

The Royal Decree 1367/2007 sets quality objectives in Annex II. For a residential land sector in existing built-up areas (Table A): 65 dB by day, 65 in the evening and 55 at night. Inside the home (Table B), which is what is measured in the neighbour's bedroom: 45/45/35 in living rooms and 40/40/30 in bedrooms.

Each city sets the periods; the Community of Madrid Ordinance divides the day into daytime (7:00–19:00), evening (19:00–23:00) and night-time (23:00–7:00). Between 65 by day and 55 at night there are 10 decibels, which the ear perceives as twice the noise: what at eleven is a nuisance, at one is grounds for a complaint.

3. Your generator already has the dB on the label

Generators are no exception: Directive 2000/14/EC, transposed in Spain by Royal Decree 212/2002 (BOE-A-2002-4099), requires the guaranteed sound power level of generators under 400 kW to be declared. That figure appears on the label and in the technical data sheet. Typical values, measured at 7 metres — the standard distance in data sheets:

Two rules of physics explain almost all conflicts: every +10 dB is perceived as twice the noise and sound drops by about 6 dB every time you double the distance. A 72 dB open-frame at 7 metres sounds like 66 at 14 and 60 at 28: moving the equipment away from the neighbour's patio is worth more than any barrier.

4. The municipal ordinance: the text that actually fines you

National law provides the framework, but your council draws up the fine. Search for 'noise pollution ordinance' plus your municipality: there you'll find the decibels per zone, the hours and the penalties. As an example, the Seville Ordinance: 'Generators and transformers must be installed in such a way that they are suitably insulated against noise and vibrations'.

Note the word insulated: it doesn't say 'silent', it says insulated against noise and vibrations. Vibrations travel through the building structure and wake the neighbour even when the air is quiet.

5. The homeowners' association: the fast route is not the fine

If the noise comes from a neighbour — or you cause it — the shortest route is the Horizontal Property Act. Article 7 prohibits carrying out in the home activities that contravene the general provisions on nuisance, unhealthy, harmful, dangerous or illegal activities:

  • Request from the chairperson, on their own initiative or that of any owner, with a warning of legal action.
  • Action for cessation if it persists and the general meeting authorises it: the judge may order immediate cessation as an interim measure, 'under warning of committing the offence of disobedience'.
  • Judgment which, in addition to cessation and compensation, may deprive you of the use of the home for a period not exceeding three years.

A badly placed generator can cost you the fine, compensation and, in the worst case, the use of your own home.

6. How to use it without sleeping with the penalty hanging over you

  • Never indoors, nor in the garage, nor in the building's storage room. Carbon monoxide kills before the fine does; a battery-powered CO detector per floor is the cheapest measure in the kit.
  • Move it away and direct it. The 6 dB per doubled distance work in your favour; point the exhaust outwards and never against a wall that reflects sound.
  • Anti-vibration base. A rubber mat cuts the path of vibration into the structure — Seville's ordinance requires it —: anti-vibration mats.
  • Timing. From 23:00 to 7:00 the target drops from 65 to 55 dB: that's when the same nuisance becomes a case file.
  • Eco mode and actual load. Data sheets declare noise at quarter load; with the equipment at maximum watts it rises. For lights and chargers, a silent inverter generator performs the same with half the noise.
  • If you live in a flat, don't buy an engine. A power station provides power without noise, without fumes and without arguments with the homeowners' association.

7. The most costly mistakes

  • Believing that domestic activity is always allowed: the law excludes you only 'within tolerable limits' according to your ordinance.
  • Looking only at the watts in the shop: if the data sheet doesn't give dB(A) at 7 m, it's equipment you don't want in a building.
  • Installing it right against the party wall: noise and vibration for the same neighbour, and the ordinance expressly mentions it.
  • Forgetting CO and fuel: safety is covered in the blackout kit guide and the first decisions in what a blackout taught us.

8. Where to start today

If you don't have a generator, first decide whether you really need one: for a flat, the comparison between generator and power station is conclusive. If you already have one, take out the data sheet, note the dB at 7 m, count the metres to the neighbour's patio and look up your municipal ordinance.
