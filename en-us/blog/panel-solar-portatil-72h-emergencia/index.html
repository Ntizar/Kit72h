Portable solar panel for emergencies: 3 types and 5 mistakes (2026)

📅 2026-10-01 · ⏱ 7 min min read

When the power goes out on your street —or across half your neighbourhood—, the bottleneck is rarely food or water: it's energy. Your phone dies, the router goes down and the fridge stops keeping things cold. A portable solar panel is the cleanest way to replace that energy for as long as the blackout lasts: no petrol, no noise, no fumes and no need to wait for fuel deliveries. That said, buying “a panel” blindly is the best way to end up with something useless just when you need it. Here we explain which type you need, how much energy it really delivers and which mistakes to avoid.

What a portable solar panel is (and what it is not)

A portable solar panel is a surface of photovoltaic cells, usually foldable, that converts sunlight into direct current electricity. You cannot use that electricity directly at home: you have to store it in a battery (a power station or a battery bank with a charge controller) and from there draw the 220 V or USB ports that power your devices.

  • It is not a generator: on its own it will not run a washing machine or a tabletop fridge. It needs a battery in between.
  • It only produces during the day: the energy is generated in daylight and consumed at night, so system design is half the battle.
  • It does not depend on the grid or fuel: as long as there is sunlight, there is charge. That is its advantage over a fuel generator.

How much energy you really need

Before you look at watts, ask yourself the right question: which devices do I want to keep running, and for how long? The table below gives approximate consumption and runtime figures for a 500 Wh battery (half a kilowatt-hour of usable capacity), a common size for home power stations:

DeviceApproximate consumptionRuntime with 500 Wh
Phone (full charge)10–15 Wh per chargeDozens of charges
Laptop50–70 Wh per chargeAbout 6–8 charges

Notice the pattern: lighting and connectivity are cheap to maintain; the fridge is expensive. If your priority is staying informed and reachable, a small panel is enough. If you want to keep food fresh, you need a considerably bigger system. For the full calculation by scenario, take a look at how many watts a portable solar panel really needs.

The three types of panel (and which one is right for you)

  • Rigid monocrystalline: more watts per euro and more efficient, but it takes up space and does not fold. Worth it if you are going to install it permanently on a balcony or in a van.
  • Folding travel panel (PET or ETFE): folds like a wallet, weighs only a few kilos and fits in a car boot or a cupboard. It is the star format for an emergency kit, because what matters is not having a panel, it is having it where you are when the power goes out.
  • All-in-one (panel + built-in battery): total simplicity and a good companion for the sofa or a camping table, although the battery tends to be smaller.

When comparing, look beyond the watts: cell type (monocrystalline versus polycrystalline), water resistance (IP rating), the connectors it comes with (MC4, XT60 or a universal DC cable) and, above all, whether it is compatible with the battery you already have. A great panel that will not connect to your battery is worthless.

How much a panel produces per day (the figure nobody advertises)

The watts in the catalogue are instantaneous power under laboratory conditions, not daily energy. In practice, in Spain a well-oriented 100 W panel usually delivers around 300–500 Wh per day in summer, and considerably less in winter, under overcast skies or with partial shade. The useful rule: assume real output well below the figure on the box and design with a margin. A cloudy day is not a panel blackout, but it does cut output a lot.

How to get the most out of it on the day of the blackout

  • If there is advance warning, charge the battery beforehand: starting with the battery at 100 % buys you half a day of leeway.
  • Orientation and angle: facing the sun, at an angle of about 30–45°. A panel in shade produces close to zero, not “a little less”.
  • Time of day: it produces mainly from 10:00 to 17:00; that is the window to use for charging.
  • Order of priority: first information and communication (phone, router, radio), then lighting and, if there is any left over, the fridge.
  • Switch off and disconnect: devices on standby also draw power; switching off what you do not need can extend runtime by several hours.

The five mistakes that make your panel useless

  • Buying a panel without a battery and discovering that it will not run anything at 220 V.
  • Looking only at the watts and forgetting the battery's Wh: the system is panel plus battery.
  • Leaving the panel behind a window pane: glass cuts a large part of the output.
  • Storing the battery discharged for months; the cells will suffer when you need them most.
  • Not checking connectors and compatibility until the day of the blackout.
