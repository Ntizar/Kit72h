Emergency kit for a baby: what to keep at home, by blocks (2026)

📅 2026-10-10 · ⏱ 5 min min read

The power goes out at three in the morning, and what weighs most isn't the flashlight: it's the baby waking up. In a home with small children, the 72 hours of self-sufficiency recommended by the European Union Preparedness Strategy aren't measured in cans or batteries, but in bottles, diapers and safe water: things that, without power and running water, suddenly stop being automatic. This is the list for putting together a baby's emergency kit without duplicating anything and without overspending, because a good part of it is already at home.

The good news is that a baby doesn't need a giant separate kit: they need the family kit to have a drawer (or a bag) with their name on it. The Baby Kit page brings together 32 essentials at an estimated cost of around 90-170 €, and several of those products are already part of your daily routine — what you need to do is rotate them, not buy twice.

1. Eating and drinking without power

This is priority number one and has three parts:

  • Water for preparing formula. A baby counts as one more person: to the 3 liters per person per day you add the water you need for hygiene. Store water in closed bottles and have a purification method — purification tablets approved for use with infants, dosed exactly according to the instructions. With safe water, formula is prepared just as always.
  • Backup milk. If the baby takes formula, keep an unopened container of the same type they take now, with the best-before date clearly visible, and check expiry every three months. If breastfeeding, the item is a manual breast pump (it doesn't depend on power) and storage bags. Any guidelines for storing expressed milk are set by your midwife or pediatrician: write them in the kit folder, don't rely on memory.
  • Heating without electricity. A chemical bottle warmer works without gas or a plug in just a few minutes. A more homemade alternative: fill a wide-mouth thermos with hot water before the power goes out.

A public health detail worth remembering in any scenario: AESAN advises against raw milk without first boiling it because of the risk of pathogenic microorganisms. If an emergency puts you in front of a neighbor with cows and no power, that doesn't change.

2. Hygiene without running water

When tap water doesn't come through, the diaper-changing routine gets complicated — but not so much if the supplies are together:

  • Backup diapers (a pack of 20-25 in the current size) and cleaning wipes as a plan B for washing.
  • Watertight bags for dirty clothes and used diapers: without water, smell is the first problem and the bags contain it.
  • Washing bottles and nipples: if there's no mains water, wash with purified or boiled water and mild soap, and sterilize with cold-water tablets. You don't need electricity, you need a plan.
  • Barrier cream and mild soap in bar form — the bar lasts for months and doesn't melt like gel.

3. Health, temperature and documents

This is where an emergency gets expensive if you're not prepared:

  • Digital thermometer, oral rehydration solution with dosing syringe (for vomiting or diarrhea, the dosage is set by the instructions or your pediatrician) and nasal aspirator with saline solution: a cold that is a nuisance at home weighs more in a blackout without heating.
  • The watertight pouch with the health record, vaccination card and the baby's ID or passport, inside the kit — not in a drawer in another room. Include a child ID card with name, date of birth, allergies, medication and two contact phone numbers that don't depend on your mobile phone.
  • Favorite small toys and backup pacifiers in a watertight bag. It isn't a luxury: it's what separates an acceptable night from an impossible one.

4. Warm clothing and evacuation

Cold and leaving are the other two fronts:

  • Spare warm clothing: a sleep sack or one-piece pajamas in the current size plus one in the next size up, a hat, mittens and a children's emergency thermal blanket (or a small fleece blanket if you prefer). Babies lose heat faster than adults.
  • Ergonomic baby carrier: if you have to leave in a hurry, the baby carrier leaves your hands free for the backpack and for the other child. It's worth trying it out in daylight, not for the first time at three in the morning.
  • The rest of the scenario is covered by the household plan: meeting point and who picks up each child, and the evacuation bag ready by the entrance.

The four common mistakes

  1. Buying formula “just in case” of a different type from the one the baby takes now: they may reject it exactly when there is no alternative. Store exactly the same one.
  2. Spreading it around the house: if you have to leave in two minutes, something gets left behind. One drawer or one bag, all together and labeled.
  3. Not writing anything down: allergies, feeding schedule, pediatrician's phone number, where the backup supplies are. In a panic, nobody remembers anything.
  4. Relying only on the mobile phone: your only flashlight and source of information. A separate flashlight and a battery-powered radio solve what the phone can't.

The baby kit isn't put together in a single Sunday shopping trip: it's reviewed every three months to change diapers for size and check expiry dates, just as you go over the age-based checklist for babies and children and the basic 72-hour kit that supports the rest of the household. If you only do one thing today, make it this: put three days' worth of diapers, an unopened can of formula and the pouch with the documents into a bag. The rest will follow.
