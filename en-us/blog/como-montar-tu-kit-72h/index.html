How to build your 72-hour emergency kit: the complete guide (2026)

Since March 2025, the EU Preparedness Strategy has recommended that every household keep supplies to be self-sufficient for 72 hours. And it is not just a desk document: with the 2024 DANA storms, prolonged power outages and heatwaves that leave millions without power, assembling a 72h kit is the cheapest and most effective self-protection measure you can take today.

You don't need to be a survivalist or spend thousands of euros. A decent basic emergency kit costs between 60 and 120 € and fits in a medium-sized backpack. In this guide I explain exactly how to put it together, step by step, without hassle.

Step 1: choose the scenario that worries you

Before buying anything, ask yourself what type of emergency is most likely in your area. In Spain the most frequent are:

  • DANA storm or flooding: torrential rains that cut off supplies, leave areas without power and isolate entire neighbourhoods. The Mediterranean coast is especially vulnerable.
  • Prolonged blackout: cold spells that drive up electricity demand and cause outages lasting hours or days.
  • Heatwave: extreme heat leaves many communities without water and hospitals become overwhelmed.
  • Wildfires: especially in summer, they can cut off access to your area and leave it without electricity.

The basic 72h kit covers all four scenarios. If your main risk is a specific one, you can add a specialised kit such as the blackout, DANA or wildfire kit.

Step 2: water — what you can't improvise

Water is the most critical resource and the hardest to replenish after an emergency. The rule set by the EU and Civil Protection is clear: 3 litres per person per day for drinking alone. For 72 hours, that is 9 litres per person.

For a family of four you need to store at least 36 litres of drinking water. The right strategy combines three elements:

  1. Bottled water or water in jerrycans: buy 6 litres per person to start with. Store it in a cool place away from direct sunlight.
  2. Purification tablets or a filter: as a plan B, if the supply is cut you can purify tap or river water. Micropur tablets or filters such as LifeStraw are cheap and weigh next to nothing.
  3. A hydration kit: oral rehydration sachets. During heatwaves or floods, dehydration is a real risk.

Replace the water every 6 months — set a reminder on your phone when you assemble the kit. If you don't want to think about it, the 7-day Swiss reserve includes 9 litres per person with an expiry date and automatic rotation.

Step 3: food you can eat without cooking

In an emergency, there may be no gas or electricity for cooking. The food in your 72h kit must be non-perishable, ready to eat and require no refrigeration.

The essentials include:

  • Canned goods: pulses, fish in oil, vegetables. Include a manual can opener (don't forget it).
  • Energy bars and nuts: calorie-dense, lightweight and with no problematic expiry.
  • Freeze-dried meals: military-style rations that only need hot water. Ideal if weight matters to you.
  • Eat what you like: if you force yourself to eat something you can't stand, you'll reject it when you need it most. The test is simple: would you eat it on a normal Sunday?

For 72 hours, allow between 2,000 and 2,500 kcal per person per day. A basic 72h kit already brings together the most practical options, but if you want extra margin, the 30-day kit expands the pantry using the same logic.

Step 4: light and power — don't underestimate the dark

When the power goes out, it's not just the darkness. It's not being able to call on your mobile, not having heating, not being able to see whether there is water, not being able to open the car door with the electric key. Light and power are critical.

The minimum viable setup:

  • A headlamp per person: it keeps your hands free and is the most useful thing after an earthquake or flood when you have to move with your hands occupied.
  • A power bank of at least 20,000 mAh: charges your mobile 4–6 full times. A 10,000 mAh power bank falls short over 72 hours.
  • A battery or dynamo radio: during a general blackout, the internet may be slow or unavailable. A radio is your link to the authorities. A dynamo one also works if the batteries run out.
  • Spare batteries: buy a pack of AA and AAA batteries and store them with the kit.

If a blackout is your main fear, add the electricity kit (60-200 €) or go straight for the professional power kit with SAI, solar panels and LFP battery.

Step 5: basic first-aid kit — what you need to know

You don't need a combat first-aid kit. With the basics you cover 90% of situations:

  • Dressings: sterile gauze, bandages, medical tape, tweezers and nitrile gloves.
  • Antiseptic: chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine for cleaning wounds.
  • Painkillers and fever reducers: paracetamol and ibuprofen (two different active ingredients, in case one doesn't work for you).
  • Regular medication: if someone in the household takes chronic treatment, keep at least 7 days' supply. In an emergency, the pharmacy may be closed for days.
  • Anti-diarrhoeals: diarrhoea in an emergency without access to clean water can be serious. Loperamide + oral rehydration solution.

A kit for older people includes these same items with adapted doses, but the basic first-aid kit works for any adult.

Step 6: documents and money — paper before the paper runs out

In a real emergency, offices close, ATMs don't work and card payments may be impossible if power and network go down. Prepare:

  • Stamped photocopies of DNI, NIE and passport of every family member. If you can, get them certified at your town hall.
  • Deeds and policies: insurance, mortgage, rental contract. A digital copy on a sealed USB drive.
  • Contact list: GP, pharmacy, car insurance, electricity and gas company. Written on paper: don't rely on your phone's contacts.
  • Cash: small notes (5, 10 and 20 €). Without ATMs or online commerce, cash is the only way to pay.

The advice from Spanish Civil Protection is to create an emergency folder with all of this together. If you don't want to put it together yourself, the evacuation kit includes a dedicated document organiser.

Step 7: the backpack — not just any backpack

The container is as important as the contents. Look for a backpack with these features:

  • Minimum capacity: 30 litres. Less is not enough for 72 hours and more than one person.
  • Waterproof or with a rain cover included: if it rains, the water shouldn't soak the contents.
  • Padded straps and a waist belt: if you have to walk with it, the belt distributes the weight and saves your back.
  • Reflective: if you have to move at night along a road, make sure you can be seen.
  • Discreet colour: it doesn't have to be black, but avoid bright colours that identify it as an emergency backpack.

You can reuse a backpack you already have. If not, a second-hand hiking backpack is enough: you don't need expensive gear.

Mistakes everyone makes

After analysing hundreds of user questions, these are the most common mistakes when assembling a 72h kit:

Buying food you won't eat

The most common mistake. If nobody in your household eats canned tuna, don't buy it just in case. In an emergency you'll be eating what you have for three days straight. Stress doesn't improve your palate.

Forgetting the can opener

It sounds like a joke, but it isn't. Thousands of cans without a can opener are food that goes to waste. Put it in the top layer of the backpack, visible.

Storing it in the storage room

The kit has to be where everyone in the household knows about it. If only you know where it is and you end up in hospital, the kit is useless.

Setting an expiry date and not checking it

Set a reminder every 6 months. Replace the water, check the batteries and the food. An expired kit is worse than having no kit.

Not preparing the children

Children need extra warmth (a sleeping bag or thermal blankets), their favourite food (so they eat something) and a comfort object (a soft toy, a book). Don't underestimate the emotional factor in an emergency.

How much does it cost to assemble the kit?

A basic 72h kit can be assembled for between 60 and 120 €, depending on whether you reuse a backpack and whether you choose generic or specialist brands. Here is an indicative breakdown:

  • Water and purification: 10-15 €
  • Non-perishable food: 20-30 €
  • Flashlight + radio + power bank: 25-40 €
  • Basic first-aid kit: 15-20 €
  • Backpack: 0-30 € (free if you reuse one)
  • Documents and cash: 0 €
