The 5 typical mistakes (and how to avoid them)

  1. Staying to defend the house when the fire is already close. Houses are defended beforehand (by clearing brush and wetting them), not when the fire front is 200 metres away: from that point on, you only evacuate.
  2. Taking the car towards the hills “because it's closer”. Fire climbs the slope and cuts off the road above first. The emergency exit is almost always downhill and downwind.
  3. Wearing synthetic clothing and sandals. It's the combination that turns a brush with heat into a burn stuck to your skin and makes evacuating on foot impossible.
  4. Shouting to warn someone far away. Smoke leaves you without air in minutes: use a whistle and a phone call, never run or shout.
  5. Believing the danger ends when the fire front is out. Embers flare up again with the wind, and burnt trees and ground remain dangerous hours later.

Quick guide

→ Before

  • Clear 10 metres around the house: brush, firewood, dry grass and hedges against the façade are the fuel that carries the fire to your windows
  • Keep metal sheets or wooden shutters to cover the windows, and the backpack by the door, not at the back of the garage
  • Write down the municipal evacuation plan and the alternative route: in a fire, the road heading up into the hills is the first to be cut off
  • Walk the exit route in your head at night: if a warning wakes you at 3:00 with smoke, you won't be able to improvise
  • Work clothes and closed-toe shoes within reach: synthetic clothing melts onto your skin

→ During

  • Evacuate as soon as there is an order or smoke in the air, carrying nothing beyond the essentials: a car is faster than a defended house
  • Close windows, doors and blinds to reduce draughts, and turn off ventilation and heating before you leave
  • If you are caught in the car: do not get out, wait with the windows closed, lights on and the air on recirculation; the car protects you better than running
  • If you are caught on foot: always move downhill and downwind (the fire climbs the slope faster than you can), head for an open area with no vegetation and, as a last resort, lie down on ground that has already burned
  • Cover your nose and mouth with a damp cloth and do not breathe through your mouth; smoke is more dangerous than the front line of flames

→ After

  • Do not return until the authorities authorise it: burned ground still has hot spots and roots keep burning underground
  • When you get there, do not turn anything on until you have checked the installations: the service line and the meter are often damaged
  • Wet down the outside and check the perimeter for hours: embers flare up again with the wind
  • Report it if you suspect the origin, and keep photos of the damage for insurance and for the local council
