Why a portable gas stove is essential when the power goes out

When electricity goes out, the first impulse is to open the fridge. But frozen food thaws in hours and canned foods need cooking to be safe. A portable gas stove is the only tool that heats water, cooks canned food and prepares hot food without depending on any outlet, battery or grid connection.

Protección Civil recommends that every household keep supplies for at least 72 hours, and in winter that self-sufficiency is not much use if you cannot heat water for tea, coffee, soup or pasta. A three-day blackout without the ability to cook becomes a hydration and malnutrition crisis within 48 hours.

Camping gas stoves used for hiking are also perfect for household emergencies. They are compact, inexpensive, run on butane or LPG canisters available at any gas station and supermarket, and heat as well as a conventional kitchen burner.

The Coleman K2 Plus is the most balanced model for household use during a blackout. Its flame-regulation system lets you go from a rapid boil to a slow simmer with the same control as a conventional gas stove. The folding grate attaches to the 220 g canister and can support pots up to 3 kg.

Most notable is its size: it fits in the 72h kit backpack (4.5 × 30 × 30 cm folded) and weighs only 500 g with the canister included. For a family that shares the K2 with the butane heater, a 220 g canister lasts approximately 5–6 hours of intermittent cooking — enough for two meals over a couple of days.

It includes a piezoelectric ignition system (no lighter or matches needed) and a built-in wind guard that improves efficiency by 30% outdoors. It works perfectly indoors, although it is recommended to ventilate the kitchen for at least two minutes every 30 minutes of use.

The Bonaverde is a basic bayonet-cartridge stove (the type of cylindrical canister that screws on top) with 3,000 W of raw power. It has no precise flame regulator or wind guard, but it boils a liter of water in 2.5 minutes — the same as an electric kettle, but without a plug.

At ~22 €, it is the cheapest stove in this comparison, and for occasional emergency use it is more than enough. The weak point is its consumption: at maximum power it uses about 200 g of gas per hour, which means only about 3–4 hours of cooking with one cartridge. For a three-day blackout, you would need at least four spare cartridges.

It also lacks a stable base for heavy pots: it is recommended not to exceed 2 kg on the grate. If your family prepares heavy soups and stews, the Bonaverde falls short in a prolonged emergency.

4. Esbit HSB-1: the ultralight tablet stove

The Esbit HSB-1 does not use gas: it burns solid fuel tablets (hexamethylenetetramine) sold in packs of 200 for ~15 €. Each tablet lasts 25–30 minutes and heats water for coffee, tea or instant soup.

Its advantage is the total absence of an open flame (it burns without a spark, at low temperature) and its ridiculous weight: 250 g without fuel, it fits in a pocket. It is ideal as a secondary stove to heat drinks, prepare dehydrated soups or water-purification tablets in a blackout scenario where hydration is the priority.

But it is not suitable for cooking canned food, boiling water in quantity or preparing dishes that require prolonged cooking. Its use is limited to "ready-to-eat" preparations or simple heating. For a complete food emergency, it should be paired with a more powerful gas stove.

Safety rules for cooking without electricity

Portable gas stoves are safe when used correctly. These rules are non-negotiable:

  • Always in ventilated areas: even if the stove is inside the house (kitchen or dining room), open a window for at least two minutes every 30 minutes of use. Combustion consumes oxygen and produces CO₂.
  • Never use the stove in a closed space: without ventilation, carbon monoxide (CO) builds up without you noticing. A CO detector is mandatory (such as the one in the blackout kit).
  • Secure the surface: place the stove on a flat, stable base. Uneven surfaces (camping tables) require a heat-resistant metal or ceramic base.
  • Keep canisters away from heat: do not store spare canisters near the butane heater or other heat sources. Heat expands them and can cause leaks.
  • Never leave the stove unattended: even with piezoelectric ignition and automatic shut-off, the flame should never be left alone.
  • Keep the extinguisher nearby: a dry powder extinguisher (ABC type) less than two meters from the cooking area is the only fire protection if something goes wrong.

Gas vs electricity: which is more practical in a blackout?

Induction stoves are more efficient (90% of energy becomes heat, compared with 55% for gas), but they require electricity. A 1,000 Wh power station can run a 1,500 W induction burner for approximately 40 minutes before needing a recharge. A three-day blackout becomes a race against time.

Gas, by contrast, does not need recharging. A 220 g canister provides ~8–9 kWh of thermal energy (the calorific value of butane is ~10.3 kWh/kg). With two canisters, you have enough to cook three meals a day for three full days, without depending on any outlet or battery.

For a short blackout (one day), the power station may be enough. For any emergency that lasts longer, gas is the most practical and predictable option.

Final verdict

The best portable gas stove for a 2026 blackout is the Coleman K2 Plus: precise flame regulation, extreme portability (500 g), automatic ignition system and moderate consumption. At ~35 € it is accessible for any household and two 220 g canisters are enough to cover three days of hot meals.
