Storing food without a fridge: what keeps and what to throw away (2026)

📅 2026-09-21 · ⏱ 11 min min read

The power goes out at 21:40. By 21:55 you've already opened the fridge “to see what's in there”, by 22:10 the freezer, and the next day you're faced with a warm fridge containing meat, fresh cheese and leftovers you don't know whether to throw away. That moment — deciding what's saved and what goes in the bin — is exactly what this guide solves: hours and temperatures measured by the Spanish Agency for Food Safety and Nutrition (AESAN) during the 28 April 2025 blackout, plus a pantry you can set up today so you don't depend on refrigeration.

And it's not a whim: the EU Preparedness Union Strategy (March 2025) asks households to have enough supplies for 72 hours. Almost all kit lists include water, a flashlight and a radio, but very few say which food really keeps without refrigeration or cooking. That's what you'll see here, along with what you can do in 20 minutes after you finish reading.

1. The hours that decide everything: your fridge without power

The first thing to understand is that the enemy isn't “the blackout”, it's the temperature each food has reached. The fridge doesn't heat up all at once when the breaker trips: it holds the cold like a cooler box, which is why what matters most isn't how long the outage lasts, but how many times you've opened the door. With the door closed, a well-stocked fridge keeps food below 5 °C for about 4 hours; a full or half-full freezer keeps things frozen for between 24 and 48 hours. With the door opened every so often, those figures fall apart on their own.

The official AESAN rules, published after the 28A blackout and included by the Ministry of Social Rights, Consumer Affairs and Agenda 2030, are these:

  • Outages of less than 4 hours (without opening the door): nothing needs to be discarded because of the blackout.
  • Outages of more than 4 hours: temperatures have probably gone above 5 °C, so throw out perishables: meat, chicken, fish, eggs, milk, fresh cheese and leftovers. Throw them out even if you cook them well: the risk isn't eliminated by the heat of a frying pan, and those foods can also contaminate others through contact.
  • Whole fruit and vegetables: they keep without refrigeration if they still look good. Cut up or already made into prepared salad: in the bin.
  • Freezer: if the food still has ice crystals, is hard and cold, it can be refrozen. If it has completely thawed but is still cold, eat it that day. If you're not sure how long it has been thawed, discard it.
  • Canned goods, soft drinks, chocolate and drinks you kept in the fridge out of habit: they don't need refrigeration and are perfectly fine.

And the rule that sums them all up: never taste a food to decide whether it's safe, and don't trust smell or appearance. If in doubt, out. Throwing away 12 euros of food is infinitely cheaper than food poisoning in the middle of an emergency, with emergency departments overwhelmed and no way to get there by car.

2. What really keeps outside the cold

Here's the heart of the matter: a prepared home isn't one with a full fridge, it's one with a pantry that doesn't depend on the fridge. These shelf lives are approximate — they depend on temperature, humidity and how you store things — but they're enough to decide what to buy extra next time you go to the supermarket:

Notice the pattern: everything that keeps without refrigeration has three things in common — it's dry, it's acidic or it's factory vacuum-packed. And everything that spoils quickly is moist and only mildly acidic: fresh meat, fish, fresh dairy, leftovers. With that criterion you can already set up half your pantry without memorising any table.

3. What you should never do (and some popular advice that's bad)

There are habits that circulate in every emergency and that are directly dangerous. These four, specifically:

  • Tasting food to see if it's still good. It's the first thing AESAN says not to do. The pathogens that grow with heat don't reliably change taste or smell.
  • Trusting the appearance of home-canned food. Botulinum toxin doesn't change colour, smell, taste or texture: a contaminated jar can taste perfectly fine. And botulism is mainly associated with home preserves of low-acid vegetables — chillies in oil, asparagus, green beans, beetroot, corn — according to AESAN documents. If a home-made jar has a bulging lid, releases gas when opened or smells odd: into the bin, without tasting it.
  • Freezing “so as not to throw it away” food that has already been out of the cold for hours. The freezer slows things down, it doesn't fix them. An open container, a lifted lid or a lunchbox that has been on the counter all afternoon doesn't become safe by putting it in the freezer: what has already multiplied is still there. Freeze what was cold; throw out what was lukewarm.
  • Leaving cooked food at room temperature “for a while”. AESAN is clear: cooked food should be eaten straight away, or kept above 60 °C, or below 5 °C. The lukewarm range is exactly where germs thrive.

And one that does work, even if it sounds like grandma's advice: heating a home-made preserve well before eating it. Public health documents recommend boiling or heating food for at least 10 minutes at 80 °C or above: botulinum toxin is destroyed by heat, so a well-boiled stew is much safer than that same jar eaten cold on toast. Even so, the best measure is not to make preserves of low-acid vegetables without a tested recipe and without acidifying with citric acid.

4. How to set up a pantry that doesn't depend on the fridge

Buying isn't the same as storing. I've seen 200-euro pantries ruined by a damp corner. What matters:

  • Where: cool (ideally below 20 °C), dry, dark and ventilated. The three worst places in a Spanish home: on top of the fridge or next to the oven, a sun-facing balcony, and a damp garage or storage room. Check AEMET warnings and, if weeks above 35 °C are coming, move delicate foods to the coolest part of the house.
  • In what: airtight glass jars with a seal or plastic containers with a rubber lid. Open plastic bags and cardboard boxes are an invitation for moths and weevils.
  • The trick almost nobody uses against bugs: if you have free freezer space, leave the packet of pulses, rice or flour inside for 48-72 hours before transferring it to the jar. It kills the eggs and larvae that came in the packaging and stops an infestation appearing three months later.
  • Cans: don't stack them in unstable towers, don't let them get wet (a rusty can eventually perforates), and don't use dented ones. Large cans always at the bottom. And a bulging can is not opened: it's thrown away.
  • Humidity: use the silica gel sachets that come in shoeboxes and mix them into jars of pasta and biscuits.
  • Date and order: marker pen on the lid with the month of purchase and the FIFO rule: new at the back, old at the front. A 10-minute review twice a year stops you discovering the problem just when you need it.

How much should you buy? Start with what's realistic: 72 hours of food that needs neither refrigeration nor cooking (the basic 72-hour kit gives you the list), then a week and, only when that rotates well, the jump to a 30-day kit. 5. Preserving without cold when the fridge never comes back

If the outage stretches beyond a day or two, you have three resources and plenty of traps. The real resources, in order of reliability:

  • Vinegar and acid: vinegar lowers the pH below 4.6, and Clostridium botulinum doesn't develop there. Vegetable pickles with vinegar are the safest and quickest home method: cut, blanch, cover with vinegar and brine, close and store in the dark. Pickled garlic and onion last for months.
  • Salt: salting and strong brines dry food out by osmosis and slow bacteria. It works with fish, pork belly and vegetables. It isn't suitable for everything: salted meat has to be cooked afterwards.
  • Drying: sun and air for tomatoes, peppers, figs, grapes or mushrooms, or an oven at minimum temperature if you have gas. It needs dry air; in humid northern areas it goes mouldy before it dries.
  • Fermentation: sauerkraut, kimchi or fermented pickles with 2 % salt acidify on their own and last for months. They need a clean jar, vegetables kept under their own liquid and a cool place.
  • Sugar: jams and preserves with plenty of fruit and plenty of sugar keep without refrigeration once opened if you cover them well and don't put dirty spoons in them.

And the traps, which are worth being clear about because they cause the most home poisonings: covering vegetables in oil (chili peppers, garlic, dried tomatoes) creates exactly the oxygen-free environment botulism needs, if you haven't acidified them first; home vacuum-sealing leftovers without sterilising isn't preservation, it's a culture; smoking by eye at home isn't a safe long-term preservation method. And if you make preserves in a water bath, bear in mind that boiling at 100 °C doesn't destroy spores: it's only reliable with acidic foods (tomato, sweetened fruit, pickles) or with a pressure cooker and tested recipes. For everything else, better to freeze, dry or not do it. And when in doubt, ask your doctor or pharmacist: they can advise you on citric acid, doses and preserves.

If you're in the middle of an emergency and the food is already warm or thawed, the important thing is to eat it in order: first the perishables that were in the freezer and have thawed, then the fridge and, finally, the pantry. 6. What you can do today in 20 minutes

None of this is any use if it stays theoretical. You can do these six things this afternoon, with what you already have or with a small purchase:

  • Put a thermometer in the fridge (a few euros) and check that it's at 4 °C or below. If it isn't, you've been paying for electricity for nothing.
  • Set aside the last shelf for things that survive without cold: canned goods, nuts, chocolate, oil. That way, when the power goes out, you know what not to touch.
  • Freeze half-full water bottles or jugs in the free spaces in the freezer: they act as a cold reservoir and, when they thaw, they're drinking water. And tell everyone at home: the fridge does not get opened.
  • Write on the fridge door the time of the last outage and a note with the 4-hour rule. In an emergency nobody remembers the figures.
  • Put an airtight jar of pulses and another of rice in the pantry, and add them to the shopping list when you use them.
  • Review your actual kit: blackout kit, home kit and, if you cook with gas or want to eat hot food, a gas stove with cartridges and ventilation. With that and a well-stocked pantry, a three-day outage stops being a food problem.

What happened on 28 April 2025 left a very specific lesson in Spanish homes: the fridge held out, water came out of the tap as soon as pressure returned, and what failed was not knowing what to do with the food. It's solved in an afternoon, and that afternoon is today.
