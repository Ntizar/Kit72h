Keeping food without a fridge: what lasts and what to throw out (2026)

📅 2026-09-21 · ⏱ 11 min min read

The power goes out at 21:40. By 21:55 you've already opened the fridge “to see what's there,” by 22:10 the freezer, and the next day you're staring at a warm fridge with meat, fresh cheese and leftovers you don't know whether to throw away. That moment—deciding what can be saved and what goes in the bin—is exactly what this guide solves: hours and temperatures measured by the Spanish Agency for Food Safety and Nutrition (AESAN) during the blackout of 28 April 2025, plus a pantry you can set up today so you don't depend on refrigeration.

And it's not a whim: the EU Preparedness Union Strategy (March 2025) asks households to have enough supplies for 72 hours. Almost every kit list includes water, a flashlight and a radio, but very few say which foods really keep without refrigeration or cooking. That's what you'll find here, plus what you can do in 20 minutes after you finish reading.

1. The hours that decide everything: your fridge without power

The first thing to understand is that the enemy isn't “the blackout”; it's the temperature each food has reached. A fridge doesn't warm up instantly when the circuit breaker trips: it holds the cold like a cool box, which is why what matters most isn't how long the outage lasts, but how many times you've opened the door. With the door closed, a well-stocked fridge keeps food below 5 °C for about 4 hours; a full or half-full freezer keeps food frozen for 24 to 48 hours. With the door opened every few minutes, those figures fall apart on their own.

The official AESAN rules, published after the 28A blackout and included by the Ministry of Social Rights, Consumer Affairs and Agenda 2030, are as follows:

  • Outages of less than 4 hours (without opening the door): you don't need to throw anything away because of the blackout.
  • Outages of more than 4 hours: temperatures have probably exceeded 5 °C, so throw out perishables: meat, chicken, fish, eggs, milk, fresh cheese and leftovers. Throw them out even if you cook them well: the risk isn't eliminated by the heat of the frying pan, and those foods can also contaminate others through contact.
  • Whole fruit and vegetables: they keep without refrigeration if they still look good. Cut up or already in a prepared salad: into the bin.
  • Freezer: if the food still has ice crystals, is hard and cold, it can be refrozen. If it has completely thawed but is still cold, eat it that day. If you're not sure how long it has been thawed, throw it out.
  • Canned goods, soft drinks, chocolate and drinks that you kept in the fridge out of habit: they don't need refrigeration and are perfectly fine.

And the rule that sums them all up: never taste a food to decide whether it's safe, and don't trust smell or appearance. If in doubt, throw it out. Throwing away 12 euros of food is infinitely cheaper than food poisoning in the middle of an emergency, with emergency departments overwhelmed and no way to get there by car.

2. What really keeps without refrigeration

Here's the heart of the matter: a prepared home isn't one with a full fridge; it's one with a pantry that doesn't depend on the fridge. These shelf lives are approximate—they depend on temperature, humidity and how you store things—but they're useful for deciding what to buy extra the next time you go to the supermarket:

Canned goods (tuna, sardines, cooked pulses, tomato)2–5 yearsDry place, no knocks or dented cans. Once opened: refrigerate and eat within 2–3 days.

Notice the pattern: everything that keeps without refrigeration has three things in common—it's dry, it's acidic or it's factory vacuum-packed. And everything that spoils quickly is moist and only mildly acidic: fresh meat, fish, fresh dairy, leftovers. With that criterion you can already set up half your pantry without memorizing any table.

3. What you should never do (and some popular advice that's bad)

There are habits that circulate in every emergency and are downright dangerous. These four in particular:

  • Tasting food to see if it's still good. It's the first thing AESAN says not to do. Pathogens that grow in heat don't reliably change taste or smell.
  • Trusting the appearance of home-canned food. The botulism toxin doesn't change colour, smell, taste or texture: a contaminated jar can taste perfectly fine. And botulism is mainly associated with home-canned low-acid vegetables—chillies in oil, asparagus, green beans, beetroot, corn—according to AESAN documents. If a home-made jar has a bulging lid, releases gas when opened or smells odd: into the bin, without tasting it.
  • Freezing “so as not to waste it” food that has already been out of refrigeration for hours. The freezer slows things down; it doesn't fix them. An opened container, a lifted lid or a lunchbox that has been on the counter all afternoon doesn't become safe just because you put it in the freezer: what has already multiplied is still there. Freeze what was cold; throw out what was lukewarm.
  • Leaving cooked food at room temperature “for a while.” AESAN is clear: cooked food should be eaten straight away, or kept above 60 °C, or below 5 °C. The lukewarm range is exactly where germs thrive.

And one that does work, even if it sounds like Grandma's advice: heating home-canned food thoroughly before eating it. Public health documents recommend boiling or heating food for at least 10 minutes at 80 °C or above: botulinum toxin is destroyed by heat, so a well-boiled stew is much safer than that same jar eaten cold on toast. Even so, the best measure is not to make low-acid vegetable preserves without a tested recipe and without acidifying with citric acid.

4. How to set up a pantry that doesn't depend on the fridge

Buying isn't the same as storing. I've seen 200-euro pantries ruined by a damp corner. What matters:

  • Where: cool (ideally below 20 °C), dry, dark and ventilated. The three worst places in a Spanish home: on top of the fridge or next to the oven, a sun-facing balcony, and a damp garage or storage room. Check AEMET warnings and, if weeks above 35 °C are coming, move delicate foods to the coolest part of the house.
  • In what: an airtight glass jar with a seal or a plastic container with a rubber lid. Open plastic bags and cardboard boxes are an invitation to moths and weevils.
  • The trick almost nobody uses against bugs: if you have freezer space, leave the packet of pulses, rice or flour inside for 48–72 hours before transferring it to the jar. It kills the eggs and larvae that were already in the packaging and stops an infestation appearing three months later.
  • Cans: don't stack them in unstable towers, don't let them get wet (a rusted can eventually perforates), and don't use dented ones. Always put the fatter cans at the bottom. And a bulging can is not opened: it goes in the bin.
  • Humidity: use the silica gel packets that come in shoeboxes and mix them into jars of pasta and biscuits.
  • Date and order: write the month of purchase on the lid with a marker and follow the FIFO rule: new at the back, old at the front. A 10-minute review twice a year stops you discovering the problem just when you need it.

How much should you buy? Start with what's realistic: 72 hours of food that needs neither refrigeration nor cooking (the basic 72-hour kit gives you the list), then one week and, only when that rotates well, the jump to a 30-day kit. 5. Preserving food without refrigeration when the fridge isn't coming back

If the outage stretches beyond a day or two, you have three resources and quite a few traps. The real resources, in order of reliability:

  • Vinegar and acid: vinegar lowers the pH below 4.6, where Clostridium botulinum cannot grow. Vinegar-pickled vegetables are the safest and fastest home method: cut, blanch, cover with vinegar and brine, close and store in the dark. Pickled garlic and onion keep for months.
  • Salt: salting and strong brines draw moisture out of food by osmosis and slow bacteria. It works with fish, pork belly and vegetables. It's not suitable for everything: salted meat has to be cooked afterwards.
  • Drying: sun and air for tomatoes, peppers, figs, grapes or mushrooms, or the oven at minimum temperature if you have gas. It requires dry air; in humid northern areas it ends up mouldy before it dries.
  • Fermentation: sauerkraut, kimchi or pickles fermented with 2% salt acidify on their own and keep for months. They need a clean jar, vegetables kept under their own liquid and a cool place.
  • Sugar: jams and preserves with plenty of fruit and plenty of sugar keep without refrigeration once opened if you cover them well and don't put dirty spoons in.

And the traps, which are worth being clear about because they cause most home poisonings: covering vegetables in oil (chillies, garlic, dried tomatoes) creates exactly the oxygen-free environment botulism needs, if you haven't acidified it first; home vacuum-sealing leftovers without sterilizing isn't preserving, it's culturing; smoking by eye at home isn't a safe long-term preservation method. And if you make preserves in a water bath, bear in mind that boiling at 100 °C doesn't destroy spores: it's only reliable with acidic foods (tomato, sweetened fruit, pickles) or with a pressure cooker and tested recipes. For everything else, it's better to freeze, dry or not do it. And if in doubt, ask your doctor or pharmacist: they can advise you on citric acid, doses and preserves.

If you're in the middle of an emergency and the food is already warm or thawed, the important thing is to eat it in order: first the perishables that were in the freezer and have thawed, then the fridge and, finally, the pantry. 6. What you can do today in 20 minutes

None of this is any use if it stays theoretical. You can do these six things this afternoon, with what you already have or with a small shop:

  • Put a thermometer in the fridge (a few euros) and check that it's at 4 °C or below. If it isn't, you've been paying for electricity for nothing.
  • Set aside the last shelf for what survives without refrigeration: cans, nuts, chocolate, oil. That way, when the power goes out, you know what not to touch.
  • Freeze half-filled water bottles or jugs in the free spaces in the freezer: they act as cold accumulators and, when they thaw, they're drinking water. And tell everyone at home: the fridge does not get opened.
  • Write the time of the last outage on the fridge door and a note with the 4-hour rule. In an emergency, nobody remembers the figures.
  • Put an airtight jar of pulses and another of rice in the pantry, and add them to the shopping list when you use them.
  • Review your actual kit: blackout kit, home kit and, if you cook with gas or want to eat hot food, a gas stove with cartridges and ventilation. With that and a well-stocked pantry, a three-day outage stops being a food problem.

What happened on 28 April 2025 left a very specific lesson in Spanish households: the fridge held out, water came out of the tap as soon as pressure returned, and what failed was not knowing what to do with the food. It's solved in one afternoon, and that afternoon is today.
