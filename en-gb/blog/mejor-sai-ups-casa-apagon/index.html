Best SAI (UPS) for home and router: 6 types by VA and watts (2026)

📅 2026-10-18 · ⏱ 6 min min read

It's 12:33 on a Tuesday and the router loses power. Eight seconds, the power comes back and that's it — until by the third week the laptop restarts on its own or the modem drops every time an RCD trips. That's the job of the SAI (uninterruptible power supply): ride out the seconds when the grid can't supply power and protect the home when the power returns.

The data point that structures this article comes from Red Eléctrica: in 2025 the mainland system recorded 12 supply interruptions, 29% fewer than in 2024, with an Average Interruption Time of 0.25 minutes, far from the 15 minutes in Article 26.2 of Royal Decree 1955/2000. The Spanish grid is excellent on average, but averages are no comfort when the outage is yours: here's which SAI you need and how many VA.

1. What a SAI does (and the three things it doesn't)

A SAI is a battery with an inverter and a transfer switch: when the grid voltage drops, disappears or spikes, it switches to battery in milliseconds and keeps supplying power — the router doesn't even notice. It also filters the spikes that arrive just as the power returns.

  • It does power: router, ONT, laptop, TV, chargers, LED lights, carbon monoxide detector: 15 to 300 W.
  • It does not power: fridge, oven, hob, washing machine or heating: over 1,000 W with an even higher startup draw, which a 1,000 VA SAI won't run for even a second. For that there is a generator or power station.
  • It is not a voltage stabilizer: it corrects voltage but doesn't provide a second of battery backup. If the power goes out, it shuts off with it.
  • It is not a generator: it doesn't create energy, it stores it. Its runtime is measured in minutes.

The difference between the first and the last is not the brand: it's how much battery you have inside. One detail that decides the purchase: the output waveform — for a router and TV any model will do, but for power supplies (laptops, audio) the ideal is pure sine wave, because some cheap SAIs have a flattened sine wave that chargers reject with a beep.

3. The VA calculation: how much you really need

SAIs are sold in VA (volt-amperes), not watts, and that's the confusion in almost every purchase. Since the relationship between the two is the power factor — between 0.6 and 0.7 in a domestic SAI — assume that W ≈ VA × 0.6: a 1,000 VA SAI runs around 600 W. The model's datasheet is what counts.

Runtime figures are ranges calculated on the stock battery (12 V and 7 Ah is around 84 Wh) and the inverter's real efficiency: they serve to compare models, not as a guarantee, and they drop sharply if you leave it discharged or in the heat. In plain terms: what a SAI buys you is not hours of a powered home, it's that your communications don't go down while you decide what to do — and that decision is in the blackout kit.

4. Winners by use case

  • Router and ONT, first of all: DC mini-UPS, the one that gives the most hours for the price. If you prefer a conventional SAI, the 600-900 VA for router and ONT ↗ is the sweet spot: it lasts as long as needed and also protects against the return surge.
  • Remote work with a laptop: 1,000-1,500 VA line-interactive with AVR: see line-interactive SAIs ↗. The AVR maintains voltage without draining the battery during afternoon dips.
  • Desktop PC that can't restart: double-conversion online, which always runs from batteries: the one in the electricity kit.
  • A home where the lights have already gone out several times: SAI with expandable external batteries: see expandable models ↗. The only way to go from minutes to hours.
  • If the goal is the whole home: it's not a SAI, it's a portable power station ↗ with a solar panel: compare first in generator vs. power station.

5. The five most common mistakes

  • Buying by VA without checking the actual watts. Work out what you'll plug in using the table above, and only then look at the price.
  • Plugging the fridge into it. Not only will it not run it: it will leave the battery dead just when you need it for the router.
  • Forgetting the battery. Lead-acid ones last 3-5 years; an expired one is a SAI that shuts off with the power. Set a calendar reminder every two years.
  • Buying it and not testing it. Disconnect the grid for a second: if the device restarts, either it's badly connected or the model isn't suitable for that load.
  • Confusing it with a voltage stabilizer or a generator. If you need hours of fridge or heating, you're in the wrong article: look at power banks and power stations.

6. Standard, maintenance and how it fits into your kit

To buy sensibly, just check that the model complies with the harmonized European standard UNE-EN IEC 62040-1:2019 (safety requirements for SAIs, in force in Spain with amendments from 2021, 2023 and 2025): it guarantees that the device is safe to touch, not that it will last for hours.

Maintenance in three lines: load test every six months (disconnect the grid for a minute), ventilation (let the SAI breathe, never in a closed cabinet) and battery replacement before the equipment complains. Golden rule: test it on a normal day, not during the blackout.
