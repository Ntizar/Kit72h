Why butane is still the smartest bet for the cold

In a winter blackout, mains heating becomes an unattainable luxury. Electric radiators use too much power to be sustainable without the grid, pellet stoves need electricity to feed the hopper and fan, and induction is simply unviable without a plug. That leaves gas — butane, propane or LPG — as the only independent, powerful and predictable heat source that does not depend on batteries or liquid fuel.

Butane in particular is still the most widespread option in Spain: the distribution network reaches 99.6% of households and a 12 kg cylinder can be found at any supermarket, petrol station or hardware store in under an hour. That, together with its price per kilowatt-hour (generally between 0.06 and 0.10 €/kWh), makes it the cheapest way to heat a home when the power goes out.

In this comparison we evaluate five butane heaters available on the Spanish market in 2026, assessing performance per euro, safety, real-world consumption and ease of use. All of them are designed for blackouts and extreme cold, not for heating a garage in summer.

1. Bondhuys 4S: the best value for money

The Bondhuys 4S is undoubtedly the heater that offers the best balance of power, price and safety. At 4,000 watts, it heats a 50–60 m² room in 10–15 minutes even when outdoor temperatures are at freezing. Its adjustable thermostat keeps a constant temperature from 15 to 30 °C without sudden cycling, and the electronic ignition system (no batteries needed) is reliable and fast.

Its most notable feature is its weight: just 3.9 kg including the cylinder, which makes it ideal for carrying from one room to another. The hot-air outlet nozzle is adjustable, so you can direct the flow toward the area in use without risk of burns, and the non-slip base prevents accidental movement.

In terms of safety, it has dual protection: tilt cut-off and oxygen depletion cut-off (alpha sensor). If the heater tips over or the O₂ concentration drops below 19%, it shuts off automatically in under three seconds. It is essential, however, to ventilate the room for at least two minutes a day to renew the air (opening a window slightly for a couple of minutes is enough).

The only point in the Bondhuys's favour over the Fiamma is price: the Fiamma usually costs about 10–15 € more. But if thermostat precision and combustion quality are priorities, the Sirocco is worth it.

Mandatory safety rules

No matter which heater you choose, these rules are non-negotiable:

  • Minimum ventilation: open a window slightly for at least 5 minutes a day to renew the air. Combustion consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide; a high concentration causes drowsiness and, in the worst case, suffocation.
  • CO detector: a carbon monoxide detector in the same room or the adjacent one. Although modern heaters have an alpha sensor, CO can accumulate from leaks in the hose or poor combustion. The detector is not an extra: it is mandatory. View CO detectors on Amazon.
  • Never use a butane heater in a sealed space: without ventilation, CO₂ concentration rises and the effects of drowsiness appear before you realise it.
  • Keep the heater more than one metre from any flammable surface: curtains, bedding, fabric furniture. The exhaust pipe can reach 80–100 °C.
  • Do not leave the heater on unattended: especially if there are children or pets. Even with a tilt cut-off, an accident can happen.
  • Store cylinders in a cool, ventilated place, never inside the home: a butane cylinder is a pressurised container; heat expands it and can cause an explosion if it overheats.

Butane vs pellets: which is better in a blackout?

A pellet stove is efficient (0.04–0.06 €/kWh) and clean (carbon-neutral CO₂ emissions), but it needs electricity to feed the hopper and the combustion fan. In a blackout, it only works if you have a SAI or a power station connected, which makes the system more expensive.

A butane heater, by contrast, is completely self-contained. It does not need electricity, lights instantly (no preheating), and the hourly cost is competitive: one hour at maximum output costs between 0.09 and 0.11 € (290 g of butane at ~0.38 €/kg). For a blackout lasting a couple of days, the cost difference is minimal and the convenience of instant on/off is non-negotiable.

If your scenario is a long blackout (more than five days) and you do not want to depend on cylinders, solar may be more cost-effective in the long run. But that is another topic: see the comparison of the best portable solar kit for home.

Final verdict

The best butane heater for a 2026 blackout is the Bondhuys 4S: maximum power (4,000 W), competitive price (~130 €), light weight (3.9 kg) and a complete safety system. It offers the best balance for an average Spanish household that wants to get through a couple of days without heating without getting too cold.

If thermostat precision is your priority and you do not mind paying about 10 € more, the Fiamma Sirocco 4000 is an excellent alternative. If the budget is tight, the Cosmos 4000 offers the same performance for 20 € less.
