Emergency kit at work: what to keep and what to do (2026)

📅 2026-09-27 · ⏱ 14 min min read

It's 9:12 on a Tuesday. You're on the third floor of a building with forty people, your backpack on the back of your chair, your car in the basement and your phone at 30%. If the fire alarm went off right now: do you know which door you exit through from your desk, where the assembly point is, and what you have in your drawer to get through the next two hours without depending on anyone?

Almost all the emergency kits we prepare are for home. And that's fine, because home is where you sleep. But add the rest of the day: nine hours at work, two commuting and only eight of sleep. Your backpack in the cupboard covers exactly the part of the day when almost nothing ever happens. This guide is for the other part: the kit that goes with you, the one kept in a drawer, and the two questions you can ask at your office today to find out whether you'd get out of the building in three minutes or fifteen.

1. Work is where you're most likely to be caught off guard (and it's not your fault)

At home you know the noises, you know which door creaks and you go out in the dark without thinking. In an office building you decide almost nothing: you depend on someone else's alarm, someone else's stairwell, whether the stairwell door is locked, whether the work downstairs has left boxes in the corridor. And you depend, above all, on habit: almost every evacuation alert you've experienced in your life was a drill, so your brain decides, without being asked, that “it'll be another test alert.” People who leave a building late almost never leave late for lack of information: they leave late because they believed it wasn't serious.

That bias has a cheap antidote called knowing your route. And your company has a legal obligation to have everything else sorted out. Article 20 of Law 31/1995 on Occupational Risk Prevention obliges the employer to analyse possible emergency situations and adopt the necessary measures for first aid, firefighting and evacuating workers, designating the staff responsible for putting them into practice. And when the building's activity falls within its scope, the Basic Self-Protection Standard (Royal Decree 393/2007) also requires a self-protection plan with a regulated structure and content, including drills, maintenance and periodic reviews.

Translated into real life: that document exists, it's in a management or prevention drawer, and you haven't read it. It isn't a company secret: it's information you can ask for. The INSST (Instituto Nacional de Seguridad y Salud en el Trabajo) has published the reference guide on emergency plans and self-protection plans, and there you find the detail that fails most in practice: the plan is written once and forgotten, when what keeps it alive is drills and reviews.

2. The kit that goes with you: nine things that fit in a drawer

You don't need to turn your desk into a bunker. The idea is to cover the two hours after an incident without depending on anyone: water, light, battery, your medicines and footwear for walking if you have to get home on foot. All of this fits in a 40 cm drawer or in a cloth bag hanging from the seat.

3. The self-protection plan: it exists, it's mandatory and nobody has read it

Here's the biggest shortcut in this guide. You don't have to design anything: someone in your building has already done it, and all you need is four questions and a three-minute walk.

  • Where is the plan and when was the last drill? The INSST reminds us that evacuation drills must be carried out at least once a year and that taking part in them is how errors are detected before they become serious. If your company hasn't held one since before the pandemic, you already know something useful.
  • Who is the emergency coordinator for my floor and who is my evacuation warden? When the alarm goes off, someone has to do a headcount. If you don't know who that is, you're one of the people nobody is going to count.
  • Where exactly is the assembly point? Ask for the address, not “across the street.” And check that it isn't somewhere where the wind leaves you breathing the building's smoke.
  • Which two evacuation routes apply to me from my desk? Always two: if the main one fills with smoke, the second has to be in your head, not just on the green sign.
  • How do you alert a deaf colleague or someone with reduced mobility? A deaf person cannot hear the alarm (you need visual alerts or someone to tell them), and a stopped lift leaves wheelchair users with no way out. There is a solution —refuge points, visual alarms, designated assistance— but it only works if someone knows their name in advance. In many buildings that person is a visitor or a colleague who is just passing through.

The walk is the rest: today, leave your desk and go down to the street following the evacuation route, counting doors and looking at where the exits you never use are. It takes three minutes. The same route, with smoke, in a hurry and with forty people going down, is not the same. And while you're at it, check something you can actually fix: that your backpack, your boxes or your scooter aren't blocking an evacuation route, because routes must always be signed and clear.

At home the logic is the same and you prepare it the same way: if you haven't done it yet, start with the family emergency plan and not with buying things. And if someone in your household depends on medical equipment, the finer detail is in monitoring and chronic conditions without power.

4. The alarm goes off: how to get out of a building without leaving anyone behind

Self-protection plans and INSST guides agree on the essentials, and almost all the mistakes seen in a real evacuation are on this list:

  • Never the lift. In an emergency it stops exactly where you don't want it to. If you're already inside and it stops, press the alarm or door-open button, call 112 from inside the lift and say which floor you're on: 112 answers even if you have no signal from your operator and even if your phone has no credit.
  • Leave the work, but take your key and phone. Not the laptop: it's heavy, gets in the way and is useless at the assembly point.
  • Go down on the correct side and don't cut across against the flow: a good share of the jams on an emergency stair are people going up to look for someone.
  • If there's smoke, stay low. Hot smoke collects above. A damp handkerchief over your mouth doesn't protect you from carbon monoxide, but it traps some particles and gives you seconds of visibility.
  • Close doors as you leave. A closed door delays the fire by minutes that matter a lot; leaving them open gives the corridor to the smoke.
  • At the assembly point, count your people. And here's the advice that prevents the most problems and almost nobody practises: agree in advance who is responsible for whom. You, with the new colleague, the intern or the visitor who doesn't know the building; someone else, with whoever moves more slowly. If nobody is responsible for anybody, everyone assumes.
  • Don't go back in. Not for your backpack, not for your phone, not for your car, not “just a second.” It's the classic mistake in every real evacuation, and in fires with casualties there is almost always someone who went back.

If you can't get out: make yourself visible and make sure you're counted

There is a case almost nobody practises and that happens more often than it seems: the exit is worse than your spot. The corridor full of smoke, the stair unusable, an emergency door that hasn't been opened in two years. Then the right decision is to stop trying to get out.

  • Close the door and seal the gap with a wet towel or a piece of clothing. You buy time and keep smoke out of the room.
  • Call 112 and say three things: street and number, floor, and which façade you're on (“fourth floor, window facing the square”). That phrase is what gets them to find you sooner.
  • Make yourself seen: a light-coloured garment showing from the window, your phone flashlight if it's dark, and the whistle if you have it. And shout to let people at the assembly point know: from outside they can tell the fire service which floors are occupied.
  • Don't jump from the window as a general rule. On upper floors it's an almost certain injury, and smoke moves faster than it seems.

That same pattern —close, seal, alert and make yourself visible— is the one used when the danger is outside rather than inside: smoke from a nearby fire, chemical smoke from an industrial estate, a leak. The difference is that then the order comes from Protección Civil, via ES-Alert or radio: if they tell you not to go out, you don't go out, even if your car is in the basement and you have a meeting at ten.

5. When the emergency is outside the building: blackout, red warning and basements

Not everything that happens to you at work starts inside. A long power cut, a DANA with a red warning or a Protección Civil alert on your phone arrives with the building intact and the problem outside: no train, no bus, no lift and the car park turned into a trap.

  • The ES-Alert notification is the Protección Civil National Alert Network: it sounds on your phone even if it's on silent, it targets phones in a specific area and it can't be switched off. That loud, flat tone is not a hoax or a test: it's the official instruction. Read it in full before you move and do what it says (stay put, don't go out, don't drive).
  • Never go down to the basement to get your car with an orange or red warning, or when water is already running down the ramp. Garage ramps are among the first places to flood and where the most people are rescued. If water rises up the ramp, you go up to the highest floor.
  • Blackout in the office: phone or drawer flashlight, never candles or lighters (and no combustion appliance in an enclosed space). If the building has no generator, the lifts stop: don't use them for even a second after a cut. And if there is a SAI or UPS, what really deserves continuity is the router and the phone: shut down sensitive equipment so it doesn't suffer the surge when power returns, just as you do at home.
  • Communicating when the network is saturated: send a text with the essentials instead of calling. On a congested network a short message usually gets through before a call, and it lets you say where you are and that you're okay. The details are in communicating without a mobile network.
  • Walking back? It can be the best decision and also the worst. With a red warning, don't move: fifteen centimetres of moving water can sweep a person away, and underpasses and dry riverbeds fill in minutes. Once the warning is over, go through places you know, in closed shoes, and tell someone your route and expected arrival time.
  • The commute is part of the kit too. If you drive, don't depend on the office: water, a blanket, a flashlight and a powerbank live in the car all year. The detail is in the car kit, and the lessons of 28A —network down, card terminals dead, people trapped in tunnels— in what we learned from the 28 April blackout.

And if it drags on and you have to get home without transport, the reference is the evacuation kit: what to take if you have to leave on foot and spend the night away. If you live alone, add one more layer: the plan for living alone starts with deciding who will call you if something happens.

6. What you can do today, in fifteen minutes

  1. Put five things in the drawer: water, bars, a headlamp, a powerbank with a cable and your medication. With that you're no longer helpless in a four-hour outage.
  2. Ask about the plan and the assembly point. An email to occupational risk prevention is enough: it's information your company has and that you can request.
  3. Walk the route from your desk to the street and count doors. Notice where the second exit is.
  4. Write down five phone numbers on paper and keep them in your wallet; and turn on AEMET alerts on your phone if you haven't already.
  5. Set a calendar reminder every six months to check expiry dates and recharge the powerbank. A kit that isn't checked is a box of old things.

And the mistakes that repeat, so they don't happen to you:

  • Confusing “I've thought about it” with “I've done it.” Thinking about where you'd get out is not the same as having walked it once.
  • The powerbank always flat. It's no use if it had gone six months without charging on the very day the power goes out.
  • Deciding on the spot who helps whom. You decide beforehand, without rushing, and in writing if necessary.
  • Going out with a red warning “to get home sooner.” That is exactly the moment to stay still and on high ground.
  • Going down to the basement to get the car. We repeat the advice because it's the mistake that puts the most people in hospital in every DANA.

A work kit isn't bought all at once and you don't need to spend 200 euros: it's nine objects, four questions and three minutes of walking slowly and looking at doors. Today, on your coffee break, you can do the five things on the list. Tomorrow, if the alarm goes off, you'll know it isn't just another alarm.
