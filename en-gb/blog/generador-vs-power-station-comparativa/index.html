Generator vs power station: 2026 comparison for a blackout

📅 2026-10-12 · ⏱ 6 min min read

On 28 April 2025, at 12:33:30, the mainland electricity system dropped to zero. The Committee for the Analysis of the Circumstances Surrounding the Electricity Crisis of 28 April —created by the National Security Council and whose report the Council of Ministers approved on 17 June 2025— concluded that the origin was multifactorial, ruled out a cyberattack and placed the full restoration of supply on 29 April at 14:36. In many homes the power came back much earlier; the problem was everything that remained unpowered in the meantime: the fridge, the boiler, the entryphone, the water pump.

That is why the buying question is no longer 'what am I missing from the kit', but how much power you need and, above all, where you are going to put it. That is where two very different answers intersect: a fuel generator or a power station (a battery unit with an inverter and, optionally, a solar panel). We compare them using Spanish data —power, runtime, cost per hour, noise and regulations— so you can decide before the next outage, not during it.

What you need to power (do this before looking at prices)

Power is measured in watts and gets used up very quickly. A rough practical rule for the first 72 hours:

  • 100-200 W: router and ONT, phone chargers, three or four LED lights, carbon monoxide detector. Anything in this article can power that.
  • 300-700 W: laptop, TV, kettle, microwave occasionally, vacuum cleaner.
  • 1,000-2,000 W: domestic fridge at startup, oil-fired boiler (circulation pump only), well pump.
  • Above 2,500 W: ceramic hob, oven, washing machine, air conditioning. There, a small generator falls short and a 500 Wh power station cannot even complete one oven cycle.

With that list in front of you, the comparison becomes clear on its own.

Indicative figures for Spain, intended for comparison, not for placing an order: they assume petrol at around 1,5 €/L and typical retail price ranges. A conventional generator without an inverter is cheaper, but it delivers a waveform that damages sensitive electronics: if you are going to plug in the computer or router, insist on pure sine wave output.

Winner by category

  • City flat or apartment → power station. It does not burn fuel, make noise or emit carbon monoxide. A 1,000-1,500 Wh station and a folding panel cover a small fridge, lights and phones: see portable power stations and 20-40 W folding solar panel, the minimum so you don't run down phone and torch batteries.
  • House with a storage room, ventilated garage or garden → inverter generator. It is the only way to boil, heat or run a large fridge for days: see inverter generators.
  • Router and computer (the first thing to save) → SAI/UPS. Cheap, silent and instant: 600-900 VA UPS. It does not replace the previous two: it buys you the first few minutes while you decide.
  • Long scenario (several days) → both: station for everyday use and generator for high-demand appliances, with the panel as a safety net.

Noise: why a generator does not belong in a communal entrance

Here the comparison is won on points, and this is the figure almost nobody checks before buying. A 'silenced' inverter generator is around 58-65 dB(A) at seven metres according to its datasheet; a conventional one exceeds 70. A building entryphone is around 50-55 dB. On a quiet street at three in the morning, a generator on the balcony can be heard from the flat next door.

And the legal framework leaves no room: Article 7.2 of the Ley de Propiedad Horizontal prohibits carrying out activities in the home that 'contravene the general provisions on nuisance, unhealthy, harmful, dangerous or illegal activities', while the noise limit values for residential areas are set by the Ley 37/2003 del Ruido and its implementing royal decrees (Código del Ruido, BOE) together with each municipality's local ordinance. In practice: plugging in a generator on the balcony or in the garage of a residential building is both a neighbour dispute and an infringement. If you want the regulatory detail, it is in the Código de la Comunidad de Propietarios del BOE.

Carbon monoxide: the argument that settles the debate

The rule is short and admits no exceptions: a generator is never used inside the home, or in the garage, or in the communal entrance, or right next to a window. The engine emits odourless carbon monoxide, and serious poisonings during blackouts usually happen exactly like this, with someone trying to get through a warmer night or charge their phone in the storage room. Protección Civil and the European Commission preparedness plan agree on the same thing: distance, ventilation and a battery-powered carbon monoxide detector on the wall, one metre from the bed and away from the ceiling.

If the home is a flat, that option is not even on the table: the generator is out of the equation and the power station wins by default.

Shopping in 10 minutes

  • Small items first: basic 72-hour kit and 20,000 mAh power bank. They cost less than an hour of generator use and solve 80% of nights.
  • If you live in a flat: a 1,000 Wh power station plus a folding panel. The wattage calculation is in portable solar panel: how many watts you really need.
  • If you have a storage room or garden: a 2,000 W inverter generator with pure sine wave output, and the solar generator alternative so you don't depend on petrol.
  • What you don't buy twice: before plugging in heating, check the comparison of heaters for power cuts; the fridge and the rest of the list are in blackout kit and electricity kit.

With 28-A on the table —the official conclusions are in the non-confidential report from the Committee of Analysis— the question is no longer whether there will be another outage, but whether that afternoon you will have a working fridge or you will be knocking on your neighbour's door. Choosing between a generator and a power station is not choosing technology: it is choosing whether your home has somewhere to store twenty kilos of noisy iron… or not.
