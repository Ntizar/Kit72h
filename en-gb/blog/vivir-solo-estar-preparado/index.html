Living alone and being prepared: kit and support network (2026)

📅 2026-09-02 · ⏱ 5 min min read

More and more Spanish households have a single occupant, and emergency preparedness has almost always been written for families. Living alone changes two things: everything you need is smaller and cheaper, and almost everything depends on a single head —yours— even when that head has a fever or is in shock. This is the plan for one.

The advantage: a kit that fits in a corner

One person, one backpack: 70-100 euros starting from zero, much less if you use what you already have. Nine litres of water for the 72 hours, a first-aid kit for one body, radio, headlamp, powerbank, the folder with your documents and that's it. No shifts or shared dosages: the whole plan is as big as your backpack. In exchange, it demands a standard: if something depends on you being well to work, it has to be charged, tested and at hand. There is no second pilot here.

The real weak point: nobody finds it odd when you don't answer

In a family, if someone falls in the bathroom, someone hears it. Living alone, the enemy is not only the DANA: it is the blow, appendicitis at three in the morning, the fall on the stairs with the phone at 2% and in your coat pocket. That is why the personal plan does not start with water: it starts with the chain of someone who asks after you:

  • The contact ritual: a daily or every-other-day message with someone who notices your absence —a friend, a brother, the neighbour on the third floor—. A hello at nine, made part of the protocol. If one day it doesn't arrive, they call; if you don't answer the second time, they come or alert the emergency services. It is not emotional dependence: it is a protocol with a name and a time.
  • Keys in trusted hands: a copy at the home of someone in that chain, so they can open up if there is a need to get in quickly, and not keep it under the mat. Without this, the first response to a serious suspicion is a firefighter breaking down your door: it works, but it is slow and costly.
  • Your phone working for you: the phone's SOS/emergency set up with contacts, medical information and medical ID; and if it suits you, a watch with fall detection. They are not toys: they are your own phone's safety net. Set them up today, because on the day of the blow no one remembers menus.
  • The card that speaks for you: a card with details, allergies, treatment and who to notify, in your ID wallet and another behind your front door. When you cannot speak, your papers speak — and the ones inside are useful to whoever comes in to look for you.

The kit for one: what changes compared with the family kit

  • Food gets simpler: a single portion per day and no menus for three age groups. Canned pulses, tuna, oats, and preserves you really like: what you don't fancy on a normal Tuesday you won't fancy on a Thursday of a blackout.
  • Critical items are doubled: in a family, devices are shared; alone, if you run out of battery there is no spare phone in the drawer. Two light sources, plenty of batteries, and a powerbank with its cable compatible with all your devices.
  • Your medication is the heart of the kit: weeks' worth of supply, a treatment sheet in the folder and a reserve travelling in the backpack. If you share care with your mother or your children in another home, apply the same system on both sides.
  • An extra only you need: morale. Being confined alone has a real component of anxiety that no catalogue covers: leave a small speaker, a book, whatever anchors you in the kit. The long day is more likely than the dangerous day, and it is managed too.

Neighbours: your safety net close by

In ninety per cent of serious domestic incidents resolved in a building entrance, the first to arrive is not 112: it is someone from the landing. Two fifteen-minute actions are worth more than any gadget: setting up a small stairwell or entrance chat group (the real neighbours, not the whole building) and identifying the other people who live alone, older people included. The network that protects you is exactly the one you also protect: in a snowfall, a heatwave or a night-time blackout, the first check that saves lives is going up or down to ask. Protección Civil's self-protection guides have had this idea at their centre for years; those of us who live alone are the ones who get the most out of it.

What only happens when you decide on your own

Living alone has a specific temptation: believing it isn't necessary, that you'll manage. You manage until the day you don't. And another: accumulating clutter instead of preparing decisions. The honest test is short: if you were evacuated tonight, do you know in five minutes what you'd take, who you'd message and where you'd sleep? If the answer is a shrug, you have a full kit and an empty plan. And the plan, this time, depends only on you.

The lists tailored to a single person are in the home kit and its compact version, the basic 72h kit. And if you drive, check the car kit: for someone who lives alone, the car is at the same time transport, shelter and telephone.
