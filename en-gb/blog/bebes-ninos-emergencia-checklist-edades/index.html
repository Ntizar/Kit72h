Babies and children in an emergency: checklist by age 0-3 and 4-12 (2026)

📅 2026-09-02 · ⏱ 5 min min read

The European Union Preparedness Strategy talks about households with 72 hours of self-sufficiency, but standard lists are written for healthy adults. A baby doesn't eat tinned fabada, doesn't drink from a five-litre water container and doesn't understand why tonight we're sleeping in the hallway with torches. Preparing a household with small children isn't about buying more: it's about buying differently and distributing responsibilities according to age.

Before the backpack: the three questions

With a child at home, the plan rests on three decisions that are best made now, calmly, not the day the alarm sounds:

  • Who picks up each child if you have to leave at three in the morning. If there are two adults, one takes the baby and the other takes the backpacks and the older child. If you live alone, the order is: children first, kit afterwards; and if the kit doesn't fit, you leave without it.
  • Where the meeting point is if you pick the children up from school and can't stop by home. Schools have protocolised meeting points: ask at the school which one is theirs and don't improvise it on the day of the alert.
  • What specific needs each child has (inhalers, special diet, allergies) and where it is written down. Clinical matters are set by the paediatrician or school nurse; you just copy them into the emergency folder.

0 to 3 years: the kit for what can't be improvised

In this age range everything depends on you: food, water, temperature, comfort. The child kit travels in a separate bag inside the evacuation backpack:

  • Extra water for preparing bottles: the 3 litres per person are not enough with a formula-fed infant. With breastfeeding there's no need to stock milk, only for the mother to drink more.
  • Formula and baby food that they already take, never new brands: an emergency is not the day to experiment with an eight-month-old's gut. Individual doses or small jars are better than a large tin that spoils once opened without a fridge.
  • Nappies for 72 hours, in their current size and one size up if you're between sizes. Allow 6–8 a day: with nerves, intestines get unsettled.
  • Wipes, a changing mat for changing on any floor, barrier cream and small bags for dirty nappies: during an evacuation there's no bin to hand.
  • A comfort object: the soft toy, the blanket, the teething ring. It sounds soft and it's one of the most practical things in the kit: a comforted two-year-old is a child who walks if you have to leave; a child in panic is an extra person to carry.
  • Double change of clothes and a change suited to the actual weather of the week you're reading this, not the summer when you filled the bag.

4 to 12 years: from luggage to be carried to a team that trains

From the age of four, the child goes from being a load to being part of the team. The difference between a chaotic and an orderly evacuation at this age is called habit:

  • Their own backpack, sized to them: not the full kit, but what they can carry (5–10% of their body weight): their small bottle, a headlamp they know how to turn on in the dark, biscuits, their jumper and their toy. Let them pack it: what a child puts away, they can find.
  • Name, surname and your phone number sewn inside the jacket and on a card in the pocket. If you are separated during an evacuation, a sewn-in detail doesn't fail or fall off: it's the only thing that works without memory or a wristband.
  • Rules rehearsed as a game: if the alert sounds, I don't hide, I wait for an adult, I don't go down the stairs alone and I count my family out loud. Two practice runs a year, like the school drill.
  • The truth, according to age: at 5 you don't tell them about the catastrophe, you say that tonight the house is camping; at 10 you explain the whole plan. Children cope better with fear if they know what will happen next.
  • Real occupation for 72 hours without screens: card decks, comics, a notebook and pencils. Boredom on day one is anxiety on day two: plan it as you plan food.

What is always forgotten

  • The fridge poster with the plan: meeting point, out-of-area contact, 112. Hung at their eye level, so the older child can read it alone.
  • The vaccination record and allergy report, copied into the emergency folder: if the child ends up at a care point, that sheet is worth more than any rushed explanation.
  • That children eat and drink less under stress: offer water often, in small amounts, without forcing meals. If they don't urinate normally or are listless, seek medical advice without waiting.
  • Transport: the folding pushchair takes up space and saves kilometres with a small child; and the car seat does not stay at home no matter how rushed you are in the car.

The ten-minute test

Before you sign off on the plan, do the count with the door closed: can you leave your house with two children, backpacks and folder in under ten minutes without coming back for anything? If the answer is no, remove things from the kit, don't add more reflexes. The folder and backpacks, together and on the way to the door, not in the cupboard at the back.
