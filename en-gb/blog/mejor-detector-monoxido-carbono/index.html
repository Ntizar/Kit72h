Best carbon monoxide detector: 6 types compared (2026)

📅 2026-10-16 · ⏱ 6 min min read

It gets cold, you turn on the heater, close the room so the heat doesn't escape, and fall asleep. That scenario — domestic, everyday, typical of any winter in Spain — is the one that most often ends up in the emergency room: carbon monoxide is colorless, odorless and tasteless; it doesn't warn you, and neither does your body. The CO detector is the cheapest device in the whole house that decides whether you wake up in time. It costs less than dinner and needs no installation, no electricity and no app.

The numbers are in the official reports: according to the Toxicovigilance Programme of the Ministry of Health, carbon monoxide is the most common toxic substance in Spanish emergency departments, with between 5,000 and 10,000 poisonings a year and an average of 125 deaths annually. And the National Plan of Preventive Actions for Low Temperatures 2025-2026 reminds us every winter that cold brings heating, braziers and generators: the risk rises just when you shut yourself in most.

1. What the standard requires: the table that determines whether it alarms in time

In Spain you can buy everything from 15 € detectors to 80 € devices, and the difference is not the brand: it is whether they comply with the European standard UNE-EN 50291-1:2018 (ratified by AENOR in February 2021), which sets out the test methods and performance requirements for domestic CO detectors. A compliant device must alarm within these times:

CO concentrationWhen the alarm must sound
30 ppmNot before 120 minutes (and it must sound within that period)
50 ppmBetween 60 and 90 minutes
100 ppmBetween 10 and 40 minutes
300 ppmIn under 3 minutes

Two readings. First: the standard does not monitor low background levels; it warns before the damage is significant — that is why it won't go off with the chimney open one afternoon, but it will if the generator has been in the garage for half an hour. Second: once it triggers, the alarm will not quieten until it drops below 50 ppm: the fact that it 'turns itself off' does not mean the air is clean. For placement, EN 50292 (selection and installation) applies: every room with a possible combustion source and every bedroom. The electrical side of the same risk is covered in passive fire safety and electrical fires.

If the budget is very tight, the priority is not the detector: it is to ventilate and not heat with combustion inside the home. With that and a well-chosen flashlight, you are covered — the selection is in best heater during a blackout and in keeping warm without electricity.

4. Where to put it (and where never to)

  • Yes: in every bedroom, in the hallway that serves them and in the room where you burn anything (open-plan kitchen, fireplace, workshop). Follow the manufacturer: almost all specify 1-3 meters from the source, on the ceiling or high on a wall and away from drafts.
  • No: inside a cupboard, behind a curtain, above the extractor hood or next to a window: there the air arrives late or is renewed too quickly, and it measures badly.
  • Garage: a domestic detector is no good for a running car: you need higher-range models there and, better still, never leave the engine running in an enclosed space.
  • Maintenance: monthly test with the button, dust it every six months (recommended by the Community of Madrid) and replace it at the end of its service life, 5 to 10 years depending on the model: the device itself will warn you.

5. Five mistakes that defeat the detector

  • Relying on smell. CO has no smell: by the time you notice it, you have already been breathing it for a while.
  • Heating with the gas oven. The Ministry of Health says it literally: never use the stove or oven to heat a home.
  • Generator, chainsaw or boiler inside the home or in the garage. Combustion engines in an enclosed space are the classic source of mass poisoning.
  • A heater left on at night with the room closed. Ventilating is the cheapest measure: better to lose some heat than to have no air.
  • One detector for the whole house. Air does not travel between floors or through closed doors: at least one per floor and one in every bedroom.

6. If the alarm sounds: four steps

  1. Get out of the room with everyone — without 'checking what it is', without turning anything off and without going back in to look for anyone.
  2. Ventilate from outside and do not go back until the emergency services authorize it.
  3. Call 112 from outside: people affected, symptoms and which combustion source was running.
  4. Have the source checked by a professional before turning it back on: a badly sealed boiler or chimney will repeat the episode.

And the warning most often ignored: headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion that ease when you leave the house are the typical picture. If you wake in the early hours with your head pounding and the heating on, get out.
