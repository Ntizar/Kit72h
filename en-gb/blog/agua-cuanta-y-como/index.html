Emergency water: how much to store, how to keep it and how to make it drinkable

📅 2026-08-31 · ⏱ 8 min min read

Why water is the first thing to run out

A human can go weeks without food, but only 3-5 days without water — and in summer, or if you have to exert yourself (evacuate, salvage belongings), the margin drops to 24-36 hours. In Spain, according to data from Civil Protection, 80% of emergencies affecting households are not natural disasters but supply cuts: DANAs that contaminate water, blackouts that stop pumps, burst pipes. Water reaches the tap, but when it is cut off, it is cut off.

The European Union Preparedness Strategy (EU Strategy for Risk and Disaster Prevention, updated 2025) recommends that every household keep supplies for at least 72 hours. Water is the heaviest item and the one people underestimate most.

The 3-litre rule: broken down

3 litres per person per day just for drinking. That is the baseline figure set by the WHO and Civil Protection. But if you want to be realistic:

  • Summer / heat (>30 °C): increase to 4-5 L/person/day. Sweat, thirst and the need to wash your hands (basic hygiene in an emergency without running water) drive consumption up.
  • Winter / temperate climate: 3 L is enough if you only account for drinking.
  • Basic hygiene (handwashing, cleaning wounds, rinsing your mouth): add 1-2 L/person/day if tap water is still available for non-drinking use.
  • Cisterns and sinks: if you have advance warning (storm, DANA), fill the bathtub and sinks. That is 100-200 L of reserve for non-food use to flush the toilet and clean.

A family of 4 needs a minimum of 36-60 litres for 72 hours. That is 8-12 five-litre containers. It is more than most people plan for, and that is why in practice they run out of water on the first day.

How to store it without mistakes

Having water is not enough: you have to store it properly. Water does not expire, but the container does, and a degraded container can contaminate what is inside.

Food-grade containers (the recommended option)

5-8 L polyethylene containers are the standard. The advantages:

  • They fit well in a cupboard or under the bed (they are not round bottles that fall over).
  • They are reusable — when you change the water every 6 months, empty, rinse and refill.
  • Price: 3-5 € per new 5 L container. 20-30 € for 5 units (which covers a family's basic need).
  • Container shelf life: food-grade plastic lasts between 2 and 5 years. If the container is cloudy, yellowish or cracked, replace it.

Commercial water bottles

Useful for a quick start: a pack of 6x1.5 L or 12x0.5 L can be bought at any supermarket and fits in the kitchen. But they are not the long-term solution: they take up more volume per litre, generate more waste, and PET plastic is less resistant over time.

Water bags or watertight bags

Water bags (also called "flexible pallets" or 10-20 L "bag-in-box") are the ideal option for large reserves. They take up less space than containers (they stack), are cheap (2-4 € each for 10 L), and you can leave them half full without the material deforming. Ideal for the garage or storage room.

What you should NEVER do

  • Reuse soft drink or juice bottles to store water: the plastic in those bottles is not designed to hold drinking water long term, and sugar or lactic acid residues feed bacteria.
  • Store containers in the garage in direct sunlight: UV radiation degrades plastic and transfers flavours to the water. Always a cool, dark place.
  • Leave containers next to cleaning products: solvent vapours can pass through the plastic and contaminate the water.

Renewal: when and how

Stored water should be renewed every 6 months. Set a reminder on your phone or stick a label on the cupboard with the date of the last renewal. The mnemonic rule: when the clocks change (March and October), check the containers. Use the old water to water plants or clean, and refill with fresh tap water.

If you use water purification tablets (see below), you can extend that window to 12 months, because the chlorine in the tablets keeps the water sterile for longer.

Making water safe to drink: three methods that work

If tap water is available but not safe to drink (a DANA contaminates the supply, for example), you have three options:

1. Boiling (the most reliable)

1 minute of boiling eliminates 99.9% of pathogens (bacteria, viruses, protozoa). The WHO recommends it as a universal method. Disadvantage: you need fuel (gas, electricity) and the water has to cool before you can drink it. Ideal as a one-off emergency method, not for maintaining long-term reserves.

2. Purification tablets (the kit option)

Chlorine (such as ACCUQUICK) or colloidal silver. The process is simple: one tablet per litre, wait 30 minutes, and the water is safe to drink. They take up minimal space (a tub of 50 tablets fits in your pocket), last for years, and are the preferred option of Civil Protection as a supplement to the emergency kit.

3. Water filter (for prolonged emergencies)

Ceramic or membrane filters (such as Berkey or pump filters) remove bacteria and protozoa (but not viruses without additional chlorine). Useful if the emergency lasts more than 72 hours and you have no fuel to boil. Price: 30-80 €. Disadvantage: they require maintenance (change the filter every 1000-2000 L) and do not remove heavy metals or chemicals.

How much water do you need depending on your household?

Not all households consume the same. If you live with:

  • Babies: add 2 L/person/day (infant formula, bottles, cleaning). A 6-month-old baby needs ~200 mL for drinking + 500 mL for formula.
  • Pets: a medium-sized dog drinks 1-2 L/day; a cat, 0.3-0.5 L/day. See the pet kit.
  • Older adults: older people feel less thirsty and can become dehydrated without realising it. Make sure they always have water within reach.

Mistakes everyone makes

1. Not having enough. "There is always tap water" — not when the supply is cut off by a burst pipe. A family of 4's 36 L runs out in a day if they are only meant for drinking.

2. Storing water badly. Containers in the sun, next to bleach, or in recycled soft drink bottles for 3 years. The result is water that tastes of plastic and cannot be drunk.

3. Forgetting hygiene. In an emergency without running water, washing your hands with soap and water is the number one way to prevent disease. An extra 1-2 L for hygiene is never too much.

4. Not knowing how to purify water. Having 36 litres stored is excellent. But if they become contaminated and you do not know how to purify them, the damage is done. Always keep a tub of ACCUQUICK tablets or a filter in the kit.

Remember: storing water is not a one-off act — it is a habit. Every 6 months, check. Every clock change, verify. And do not wait for the tap to run dry to realise you have nothing.

Can you drink tap water after a DANA?

It depends. DANAs (isolated high-level depressions) usually contaminate rivers and aquifers with sediment and bacteria, but rarely affect the water treatment plant. However, authorities may issue a "do not drink tap water" notice if local pipes become contaminated. Always follow Civil Protection instructions and, if in doubt, boil the water or use water purification tablets.

How long does unopened bottled water last?

If it is in a closed commercial container (factory PET bottle), the water theoretically lasts indefinitely. What degrades is the container: PET becomes brittle over time and with UV radiation. Practical recommendation: change the water every 2 years if it is in an unopened commercial bottle.

Can you purify puddle or river water?

Technically yes, but it is not recommended unless strictly necessary. Purification with tablets or boiling removes pathogens, but does not remove heavy metals, pesticides or chemical contaminants. If you have no other option, boil the water (10 min) and then use tablets. As a previous step, filter it through a clean cloth to remove visible particles.
