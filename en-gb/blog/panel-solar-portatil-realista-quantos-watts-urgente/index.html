Portable solar panel: how many watts you really need (2026)

📅 2026-09-18 · ⏱ 8 min min read

The blackout on 28 April 2025 left half the country without power and with a phone at 2%. Most people solved it with the power bank they already had charged. But if the power goes out for several days and there is no sun (or it rains for weeks), the power bank drops to zero and doesn't recharge. This is where the portable solar panel comes in, and why almost everything sold on Amazon doesn't do what it promises.

I'm not going to sell you a 300-watt panel for your balcony. I'm going to tell you what a real panel can do — or not do — when you're left without power, and which panel + battery combo makes sense to get through a week without grid power.

The trap of advertised watts

A portable solar panel advertised as 100 watts rarely delivers 100 real watts. The reason isn't a scam — it's physics: panels are measured under standard test conditions (STC), which are 1000 W/m² irradiance, cell temperature at 25 °C and AM 1.5 spectrum. In real life:

  • Irradiance in Spain never reaches 1000 W/m² on a sustained basis (even in summer, the ceiling is usually between 800 and 950 W/m²; official AEMET and IAE data confirm this).
  • Cell temperature easily rises to 45-65 °C in direct sun: each degree above 25 °C reduces power by 0.3% to 0.5%. At 55 °C, a panel loses 9% to 15% of its power.
  • The sun's angle of incidence changes constantly; if the panel isn't perpendicular to the sun, you lose 20-40% of performance.

A 100 W panel correctly oriented on a clear June day in Spain delivers 60 to 80 real watts, and only for about 5-6 hours of the day. A 50 W panel correctly oriented delivers 30 to 40 real watts. That's the figure you need to use for your calculations.

How much energy do you need in an emergency?

Before buying, you need to know what you're going to charge. Here is the minimum most people need to keep running:

  • Phone (full charge): 10 to 15 Wh (a modern 4000 mAh phone at 3.7 V is ~15 Wh; with charger efficiency and losses, you need ~12-15 Wh net).
  • Battery-powered FM radio: 0-2 Wh (if it uses AA batteries, the panel won't charge them; if it has a USB port, 1 to 2 W).
  • LED headlamp: 2 to 5 Wh per charge.
  • Power bank you recharge: a 20,000 mAh power bank at 3.7 V is 74 Wh; with boost losses, you need to put about 90 Wh net into it.

The combo that really works: panel + power bank

A solar panel on its own isn't much use in an emergency: it generates energy when the sun is out, not when you need it. What you need is generate + store. The minimum useful combo is a 30-50 W panel plus a power bank of at least 20,000 mAh. Here's how it works:

  • You generate with the panel when there is sun (between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., preferably with the panel facing south and perpendicular to the sun).
  • You store it in the power bank, which is already part of your basic 72h kit.
  • You use it when you want: at night, in the rain, with the phone in your pocket.

The key is that the power bank has a USB input that supports the voltage and amperage coming from the panel. A 50 W panel generates 5 to 6 V at 8-10 A: you need a power bank that accepts that input (most modern ones do). If not, you'll have to use a solar controller (PWM or MPPT), which adds cost and complexity.

What panel size is enough?

For household use in a crisis:

  • 30-50 W (the minimum): charges a phone 6 to 10 times a day in summer. Recharges a 20,000 mAh power bank every 2-3 days. Fits in a backpack. This is what I recommend for most households if you're only going to charge a phone and radio.
  • 50-100 W (the reasonable option): recharges the power bank every day in summer, every two days in winter. Enough to keep phone, radio and headlamp running without worry. Takes up a bit more space but fits in a car trunk or on a terrace.
  • 100-200 W (the portable maximum): recharges the power bank several times a day. Useful if you need to power something else (a room LED light, a USB fan), but you start to need structure and a fixed surface. It's not a backpack item: it's a terrace setup.

What to look for (and what not to) when buying a panel

The current market has everything. The important thing is to tell what works from what is decoration:

  • Monocrystalline panels are more efficient (higher conversion percentage) than polycrystalline ones: a 100 W monocrystalline panel weighs less and takes up less space than a polycrystalline one of the same wattage. The price difference is minimal.
  • Folding or rigid panel: folding ones are practical to carry in a backpack; rigid ones are more durable and are installed permanently on a terrace or balcony. There isn't a big difference in performance if the monocrystalline cell is the same.
  • Built-in controller: many panels already include a PWM regulator or a direct USB connector. If they don't, you need a separate controller (5 to 20 €). Always check that it's compatible with your power bank.
  • Avoid panels with a built-in battery: the battery heats up in the sun, degrades and is also much heavier. The right approach is a separate panel + power bank.
  • Water resistance matters: look for panels rated IP65 at minimum if you're going to use them outdoors. During a DANA it rains, and you're not going to keep the panel under a roof.

Typical mistakes

  • Buying a large panel without thinking about where to set it up: a 100 W panel needs about 6-7 m² of surface to deploy (double-panel folding models measure ~1.5 x 1 m; four-panel models ~2 x 1 m). If you don't have a south-facing terrace or balcony, it ends up in the shade of a tree and is useless.
  • Not having a power bank charged beforehand: the panel generates energy when the sun is out; if you don't store it, you lose the hours without sun. The power bank is the bridge between generation and consumption.
  • Pointing the panel at the sky: the sun isn't always overhead. In winter, at 10 a.m. or 4 p.m., the angle is very low and performance drops 30-50%. Adjust the panel angle according to the time of day.
  • Covering the panel without noticing: a leaf, a coaster, a patch of thick fog. If the panel gets dirty, output drops 10-30%. Clean it from time to time.
  • Confusing watts (W) with watt-hours (Wh): the panel says 100 W: that's 100 watts when the sun is perfect at that instant. Wh are what you generate in a day (W × h). To know how many phone charges you can expect, multiply the real W by the equivalent sun hours.

What if there's no sun for weeks?

That's the real limitation of a portable solar panel: it doesn't generate when there's no sun. And in Spain, the months with the highest risk of crisis (autumn and winter) are precisely the ones with the least sun. That's why the panel is a complement, not the base of the kit. The base is the charged power bank (which you already cover in the blackout kit) and the battery-powered radio. The solar panel is what ensures that, when the sun comes back, you don't have to depend on anyone to recharge.

The EU recommends 72 hours of autonomy; the solar panel helps you go beyond those three days if the crisis drags on and the sky clears. But if all you have is a panel and not a power bank, you have nothing.

Practical summary

  • Reasonable minimum: 30-50 W monocrystalline panel + 20,000 mAh power bank.
  • Ideal: 50-100 W panel + large power bank (20,000-30,000 mAh) with a wide USB input.
  • Fixed installation: if you have a south-facing terrace, a fixed 100 W rigid panel is best: no need to orient it, it doesn't move, it generates all day.
  • Remember: the panel is not the base of the kit. The charged power bank, battery-powered radio and non-perishable food are. The solar panel takes you from 3 days to a week if the sky clears.
