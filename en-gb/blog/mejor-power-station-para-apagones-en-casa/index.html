Best power station for home power outages: 5 models compared (2026)

📅 2026-10-24 · ⏱ 8 min min read

The power goes out at eight o'clock on a Friday night in November. The router turns off, the Wi-Fi dies, and suddenly you have a home without communications, without power, and without a fridge. The first thought is your phone: 15% battery. The second is the fridge: “How long does it hold with the door closed?” The answer to both questions depends on the energy you have inside your home — and if you have none, here is the comparison of the five power stations that give the best price-to-hours ratio in a home blackout in Spain.

Context: Red Eléctrica de España (REE) reported 12 interruptions in 2025, with an average of 0.25 minutes per outage. The grid is reliable on average, but the average does not cover the scenario that matters to you: a blackout lasting more than one night, whether due to a storm, a local distribution failure, or an overload of the street transformer. The International Energy Agency estimates that European households lose an average of 20 to 40 hours of electricity per year when planned outages and extreme events are combined. That is more than a small power station can provide.

1. What a power station is and what is NOT a SAI

A power station (or portable power station) is a large battery with a built-in inverter: it provides 230 V outlets, USB-C, USB-A and, on some models, a solar input. It is the midpoint between a SAI (seconds of protection for the router) and a gasoline generator (kilowatts, but with smoke, noise and a municipal permit): more powerful than a SAI, cleaner than a generator.

  • It can run: router, small fridge, power tools, space heater, microwave, washing machine (short cycles), lights, computer.
  • It does not replace: a gas boiler (it needs gas from the grid), a continuous induction cooktop, or a residential air conditioner (it demands kW that no portable unit can deliver).
  • It is NOT a SAI: a SAI switches to battery in milliseconds without interruption; a power station is turned on manually. If you want the router not to blink even for a second, use a SAI — the power station is for when the grid does not come back.

3. The hours calculation: what each power station can run

To calculate runtime, divide the battery capacity (Wh) by the appliance's consumption (W). That simple. One detail that matters: the inverter has an efficiency of 80–90%, so a 500 Wh power station delivers in practice about 400–450 Wh of real energy.

The conclusion is clear: without at least 500 Wh, you cannot run a fridge. Without 1000 Wh, you cannot run a family fridge. With solar panels connected (the models in the third category and above allow this), a small fridge can run indefinitely on a sunny day — because the 500 Wh in the battery are recharged by the sun while the fridge consumes them.

5. The five most common mistakes when choosing

  • Choosing by capacity without looking at power. 500 Wh will not run a fridge if the inverter only delivers 300 W. The continuous power of the inverter is the limit: nothing that exceeds that number.
  • Buying a 100 Wh unit and expecting it to run the fridge. 100 Wh is for router hours, not refrigeration hours. It is like buying a bucket to put out a fire with a garden hose.
  • Ignoring solar recharging. A 3-day blackout with 500 Wh and no recharging runs out after 12 hours of fridge use. A model with solar input and a 100 W panel recharges by itself on a sunny day.
  • Confusing Wh with mAh. mAh is a unit for the internal cell, not for the complete system. A seller can put “20000 mAh” and mean only the Li-ion battery (3.7 V), which is ~74 Wh. Always look at Wh: it is the real unit of energy.
  • Not checking the weight before buying. A 1000 Wh power station weighs about 9 kg. If you need to carry it upstairs to a second floor or transport it, consider the weight. 300 Wh units weigh ~3 kg and fit in a backpack.

6. Safety, regulations and how it fits your kit

To buy wisely, make sure the power station complies with the European standard UNE-EN IEC 62133-2:2017 (safety of lithium-ion batteries) and that it includes protection against overcharge, overheating and short circuit. Models that comply with this standard carry the CE marking and are sold in the EU with a warranty.

Maintenance in three lines: recharge it at least every three months (lithium-ion batteries degrade if left discharged), store it between 20–30 °C (a hot garage in summer shortens its life) and test it every six months: plug something in and let it run down to 20% to confirm it works.
