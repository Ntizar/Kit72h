Emergency food: what to eat, how much to store, and how to rotate it without throwing anything away

📅 2026-10-02 · ⏱ 8 min min read

Why food expires before the emergency

Water is the top priority, and you shouldn't wait until you're very thirsty to look for it. Food is the second thing people forget when preparing a kit, and also the thing that spoils most often: people buy special 'emergency' cans or jars that end up in the bin because they expire. The solution is simpler: the best emergency food is the food you already eat.

Many European and Spanish guides recommend a reserve of at least 72 hours; it is not a legal obligation. The key is not to buy 'emergency food' but to rotate what you already eat: that way the reserve gets used, restocked, and doesn't turn into a museum of cans.

The golden rule: buy to eat, not to store

The most effective method is FIFO (First In, First Out). It works like this:

  1. Buy an extra of what you already eat regularly: one more packet of pasta, two more cans of tuna, one extra packet of biscuits.
  2. When you use the extra in your normal life, replace it directly in the reserve.
  3. Your emergency stock rotates itself, without you having to think about dates or expiry.

This turns an 'emergency reserve' into something that requires no management: it is simply your regular pantry with an extra 20–30%. If you already shop every week, you don't need a special shopping trip: add one unit of what you already eat and let rotation do the rest.

What people forget: salt keeps practically indefinitely and honey lasts a long time if well sealed, but neither replaces a varied diet or hydration. Salt does not 'prevent fainting': if someone feels dizzy or is dehydrated, assess the situation and seek help; do not give spoonfuls of salt.

There is no single figure: it depends on whether you have a kitchen available. If the power outage leaves the fridge not working but you can heat canned goods over a fire, you need fewer ready meals. If you have no safe way to cook, you need everything ready to eat.

The 'double packet' rule in practice

The simplest method that works requires no inventories or dates. Every time you go to the supermarket, apply these rules:

  • Canned goods: if you buy a pack of 4 cans of tuna, add 2 more. The extra ones are your reserve; don't separate them from the pantry.
  • Pasta or rice: if you use a 500 g packet a week, keep another one closed. One gets used, the other restocks.
  • Biscuits or crackers: one extra packet. They are the snack for the first day, when you don't yet want to open a can.
  • Nuts: one extra bag. They expire before canned goods, so they go first in the rotation.

With normal shopping and a little foresight, a family can have the full reserve without a huge outlay. You don't need to fill a cupboard all at once: rotation does the work.

When to check the reserve: a practical schedule

You don't need a calendar or alarms. Do the check every time the clocks change (March and October), just as you do with remote-control batteries:

  • Check dates: 2 minutes. Look at the dates on nuts, biscuits, powdered milk, and canned goods.
  • Complete what is missing: if something expired, replace it with a new one and put it at the front.
  • Restock what was used: if the reserve went down (because of a trip or journey), top it up with the next shop.
  • Check special needs: if there are babies, allergens, or restricted diets, check that the stock is still valid and sufficient.

Preparedness is a habit, not a one-off act. Checking twice a year takes less than 10 minutes and prevents throwing food away or discovering a problem when the supermarket is no longer available.

Common mistakes with emergency food

1. Buying special 'emergency food'. Freeze-dried meals can last for years, but they also cost more and don't always match what you eat. With canned goods and dry foods you already eat, you have a reserve that rotates and isn't wasted.

2. Not having anything ready to eat. If the power goes out and you don't have a safe way to cook, unopened cans won't solve dinner. Keep bars, biscuits, and nuts for the first day.

3. Storing food in a hot or humid place. Heat and humidity degrade packaging and food. Choose a cool, dry, dark place; if the pantry gets very hot in summer, find another spot.

4. Forgetting salt and hydration. Salt is useful for cooking, but it is not a medicine and does not by itself prevent fainting. In an emergency, water and medical care come first.

5. Not thinking about dependents. Babies (formula or infant milk), older people (soft or specific diets), pets, and people with allergies have their own needs. Each one needs its own reserve and check.

Do canned foods really expire?

For canned goods, the date usually indicates quality more than safety: an intact can, with no dents at the seams, no rust, no swelling, and no leaks, may keep its quality beyond the 'best before' date, but that does not authorize adding years to the date. If in doubt, discard it. But don't use it as a general rule: if the can is dented, swollen, smells bad, or gas comes out when you open it, discard it. When in doubt, don't take the risk.

Can you cook without electricity or gas?

Yes, but you need a safe heat source and ventilation. A camping stove should only be used outdoors or in a well-ventilated space; never inside a home, tent, or car, due to the risk of carbon monoxide. Without safe cooking, eat ready-to-eat foods and don't improvise with fuels.

What should you do if there are babies or allergens?

For babies, always follow the label on formula or infant milk and the preparation instructions; do not use expired products and discard any leftovers after a feed. If there are allergies, store foods labeled as free from the allergen and check the labels every time: formulations change. For medical diets, consult a professional before building the reserve.
