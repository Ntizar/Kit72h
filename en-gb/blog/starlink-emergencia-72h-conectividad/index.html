Starlink in a blackout: setting it up, powering it and whether it's worth it (2026)

📅 2026-10-01 · ⏱ 10 min min read

When the power goes out across a whole area, the first thing that fails isn't the fridge: it's information. The router dies, the phone loses data and local networks get saturated. That's the moment many people think of Starlink, SpaceX's satellite constellation that promises internet from any spot with a clear sky. This article is the practical guide: what Starlink solves in a 72-hour emergency, what it doesn't solve, how much it consumes and how to power it when there's no grid, so you can decide sensibly whether it deserves a place in your preparedness plan — or whether something simpler and cheaper is enough.

The official recommendation is the usual one: the European Union Preparedness Strategy asks households to keep 72 hours' worth of supplies, and the Directorate General for Civil Protection and Emergencies insists on keeping a reliable source of information during any incident. Starlink can be that source, but only if you're clear about how it stays powered without a socket. Let's take it step by step.

What Starlink is (and why it comes up in every emergency)

Starlink is a constellation of low-Earth-orbit satellites offering broadband internet without relying on ground infrastructure: there's no fibre to lay and no mobile towers in between. You only need three things: a terminal with a clear view of the sky, an active data connection through its network and electric power to run it. That last condition is the one to hold on to, because it's usually what's missing just when you need it most.

In an emergency scenario, that architecture has one concrete advantage: even if the power plants, substations or mobile towers in your area are out of service, the satellites are still up there. If the local grid is down but the satellite operator's infrastructure works, you can get connectivity back in minutes. It's the exact opposite of what happens with the mobile network, which depends on towers with limited battery backup and generators.

What Starlink is NOT: three wrong expectations

Before spending money, it's worth taking apart three common ideas:

  • It's not a cheap emergency plan B. The terminal is a serious purchase and the monthly fee is recurring. If you're not going to use it day to day, there's no point buying it «just in case»: you'll have spent money on a piece of kit that loses value in a drawer while your 72-hour plan is still missing the basics (water, light, first-aid kit, radio).
  • It doesn't work blind or indoors. It needs line of sight to the sky: narrow balconies, inner courtyards with high walls or dense forest degrade the signal noticeably.
  • It doesn't replace official radio or 112. For official alerts and Civil Protection warnings, the route is still the emergency radio and the national emergency channels; for real emergencies, 112. Starlink gives you additional routes (messaging, checking sources, calls over data), not a replacement.

That said, there are scenarios where it changes the picture completely: evacuations to rural areas with no coverage, work in the mountains, houses on industrial estates or in villages with poor mobile coverage, and of course long blackouts where the mobile network gets saturated with people trying to call.

The key point: how much it consumes and how to power it off-grid

Here's the question that really matters. A typical residential Starlink terminal draws around 60-75 W in active use and somewhat more than 20-40 W at rest, depending on the model and whether it's in sleep mode. Translated into a three-day blackout, with reasonable use of about 10-12 hours a day, you're talking about between 0.7 and 1.2 kWh per day… and that's where most plans fall apart, because that comfortably exceeds the capacity of almost any handheld power bank.

The realistic options, in order of practicality:

  • Power station. This is the serious option: a 1,000-2,000 Wh battery with a 220 V output gives you one or two days of Starlink plus the router, lights and phone charging. Switch it on only when you need it and put the terminal in standby to stretch the margin. The Electricity Kit gathers the selection and the article on portable solar panels explains how many watts you really need to recharge it in the sun.
  • Portable solar panel. With a well-oriented 100-200 W panel, a morning of decent sun returns a good part of a full day's consumption. It's the combination that lasts the most days, but it depends on the sky: on a storm day or with heavy overcast, generation drops — and that's where the battery cushion comes in.
  • Large power bank as a short reserve. A 20,000 mAh power bank or more with 65 W output (the 20,000 mAh model in the kit is one example) powers the terminal for a few hours: enough for a quick look at maps and alerts, not for working or leaving it on all night.
  • Car alternative (carefully). Some terminals work with 12 V vehicle adapters. Use it with the engine running or with a monitored auxiliary battery: leaving the vehicle draining the starter battery is a sure way to end up without a car and without internet at the same time.

Golden practical rule: Starlink is the last link in the power chain, not the first. First you secure water, power and heat or cooling; then communications; Starlink goes in that third layer, together with the phone and the radio.

What it really costs, no surprises

There are two separate line items, and it's best not to mix them:

  • Equipment (one-off). The terminal is around 500-700 € for the standard residential model and goes above that figure for mobile or higher-performance versions. Add accessories depending on the setup: roof or mast mount, a long extension cable if the good window is far away, and a case or transport bag if you're going to move it.
  • Subscription (monthly). In Spain the residential plan is around 45-60 € a month, with portable plans somewhat more expensive because of use on the move. Cancelling and reactivating is possible, but with a re-provisioning cost in some cases: read it before, not after.

The total for the Starlink Kit (terminal + accessories + a year of subscription) is easily a family holiday budget or more. The useful question isn't «can I afford it?», but «what other gap in my preparedness plan does this money leave uncovered?». If your first-aid kit is empty and your basic kit doesn't exist, prioritise that first; it's all in the basic 72-hour kit.

Setting it up in 30 minutes: commissioning

Home installation is simple, and the time to test it is NOW, on a good day, not with the power out:

  1. Choose a location with a clear sky. Mount the terminal on a roof, a high balcony or a large window facing a wide angle of sky. The right mounting bracket keeps the first gust of wind from bringing it down.
  2. Connect it and let it calibrate. The terminal aligns itself and searches for satellites; the app tells you whether the position is good. If the app reports poor signal at the first location, raise or move the terminal before accepting it.
  3. Power it from your backup system. Check that it starts from the power station and note how many Wh it uses in one real hour of use. That figure, measured by you, is worth more than any table on the internet for planning your days of autonomy.
  4. Test the use cases that matter to you: a family video call, checking the alerts map, messages. Check whether your home router also starts from the battery — if not, you'll have WiFi with nothing behind it.
  5. Leave EVERYTHING labelled and accessible: cables, adapters and manual in a waterproof bag with the terminal. During a night-time blackout nobody improvises well.

One safety detail: if there's a thunderstorm or a severe weather warning, disconnect the outdoor terminal. You can check the official AEMET warnings report… using Starlink itself while there's power, of course.

Redundancy: Starlink shouldn't go alone

No single system gets you through an emergency, and satellite is no exception: a tree fallen on the antenna, a sandstorm or a constellation failure leaves you with no route. Sensible preparedness stacks independent routes, in this order of increasing cost:

  1. Emergency radio — battery, crank or solar: official information without internet, at almost zero cost. The basis of the Communication Kit.
  2. Mobile phone with coverage and a data SIM — if the local tower holds up. The analysis of which data SIM survives a blackout best and the one on emergency 4G routers cover this layer.
  3. Light satellite messaging — devices such as a satellite messenger that send position and short messages with no mobile coverage: the most reliable way to say «I'm fine» when nothing else works.
  4. Starlink — full bandwidth, the last step and the most power-hungry.

The full philosophy — when to combine each route and in what order — is developed in the companion guide Emergency connectivity: Starlink, radio and satellite and in how to communicate without a mobile network. Coverage and legality in Spain

Two clarifications that save you grief:

  • Coverage. Availability of the residential service in Spain isn't uniform: there are areas with a quota or waiting list and others where the portable service has restrictions on use while moving. Check coverage with your exact address BEFORE buying the terminal; the official check is done on the operator's website and saves you paying for equipment you can't activate.
  • Installation. Mounting an antenna on the roof or façade of a shared building subjects the work to the building's rules and, in heritage areas or on accessible rooftops, to additional procedures. On isolated roofs and detached houses it's much more straightforward, but check the municipal by-law again if you're unsure.
  • Responsible use. Even though the terminal depends on a private network, in an emergency your consumption still counts: if the network is congested, prioritise short messages and voice calls over streaming. The same logic as with 112: not congesting is part of being prepared.

The four common mistakes

  1. Buying the terminal and not the power. It's the number one mistake: the blackout comes, the terminal starts up and the battery runs flat in a few hours. Work out your Wh first, then buy.
  2. Installing it on the day of the blackout. Calibration, location and cables are tested with sun and time. On the day of the emergency you only switch on what has already been tested.
  3. Forgetting the router. Starlink gives you a connection, but if your router doesn't start from the battery, the home WiFi stays dead. Check that the whole chain (terminal + router + mobile phone) is powered.
  4. Getting complacent and dropping the radio. Satellite connectivity is one more layer, not the base. The battery radio and 112 remain the backbone of official information.

In short: Starlink in an emergency is an excellent tool for anyone who already has the basic layers covered and can power it off-grid.
