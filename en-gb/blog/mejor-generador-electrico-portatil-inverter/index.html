Best portable inverter generator (2026): power, noise and the real risks

📅 2026-10-23 · ⏱ 7 min min read

What is an inverter generator and why it is not the same as a cheap one

An inverter generator starts a petrol engine, produces alternating current, converts it to direct current, stabilises it electronically and converts it back into clean alternating current. The result is a sine wave with distortion below 5%, stable even as the load rises and falls. A conventional generator with AVR delivers a dirtier wave and with the frequency fluctuating depending on what you plug into it.

What does that change in your home? That an inverter can power the router, laptop, television, boiler or the SAI of a blackout kit without any nasty surprises, whereas a cheap conventional one makes electronics restart, buzz or simply fail to start. During a blackout, almost everything you want to keep running is electronics: that is where the purchase decision is made.

The price of that difference is real: an inverter costs more per kilowatt than a conventional generator of the same power. For home use it is worth it; for construction sites and power tools, not always.

How much power you really need (and why peak power changes everything)

Every generator advertises two figures that are not the same: continuous power (what it can handle indefinitely) and starting or peak power (what it can handle for a few seconds). The motor of a fridge, a freezer or a pump does not draw its rated power when it starts: it draws 2 to 5 times more during the first second. If the peak is not enough, the generator stalls or the appliance does not start.

The correct calculation: add up the continuous consumption of what you want to have running at the same time and add the peak of the most demanding appliance, not the sum of all the peaks. For an apartment with a fridge, router, laptop and lights, an inverter with 2,000 W continuous and 2,200-2,400 W peak is more than enough. If you also want to cover a boiler, microwave or power tools, the reasonable jump is to 3,000-3,500 W.

How long it lasts on a tank (the calculation almost nobody does)

A small inverter uses between 0.6 and 1 litre per kilowatt-hour generated, depending on how heavily loaded it is. Translated into tanks: a 4-litre tank lasts 3-4 hours at half load; a 10-litre tank, 8-10 hours; a 15-20-litre tank, a day and a half with intermittent use.

For a 72-hour blackout, that is between 25 and 40 litres of petrol. And that is where the part nobody plans for comes in: storing fuel. Ethanol-free, in approved fuel cans, outside the home, and rotating it every three months (with stabiliser it lasts considerably longer). If your scenario is a week or more without the grid, the generator is not the complete answer: first compare a generator and a power station with solar panels, because you do not have to go and buy solar kWh.

What really kills: carbon monoxide

A generator emits carbon monoxide, which is odourless, colourless and kills without warning. Never inside the house. Never in the garage, not even with the door open: the gas builds up indoors and rises. Never on an enclosed balcony or in an indoor drying area. The unit goes outdoors, on a firm surface, with the exhaust pointing away from doors and windows and more than 6 metres from them (20 feet, CDC reference).

And at home, a carbon monoxide detector on the floor where you sleep. It is not an extra: it is the difference between a scare and a tragedy. We cover it in more detail in the comparison of CO detectors.

Noise and neighbours: what the rules say

A 2 kW inverter stays at around 58-65 dB at seven metres; a conventional one goes above 70. There is no specific national law for domestic generators: the municipal noise ordinance applies (typical limits of 55-65 dB by day and 45-55 dB at night in residential areas) and, if you live in a shared building, the community rules. A generator at full load on a terrace at eleven at night is an almost guaranteed complaint, and in many buildings it is directly prohibited. We cover this in generators and community rules.

The combination that works in a genuinely prepared home is a mixed one: a power station and solar panels for the small and permanent loads, a generator for what demands a sudden burst of power. Checklist before buying

  • Real continuous power (not the brochure figure) and starting peak.
  • Pure sine wave with declared distortion (below 5%).
  • Tank: from 8-10 litres to last a night without refuelling.
  • Electric start and, if possible, remote control: at three in the morning you do not want to be pulling a cord.
  • Weight and wheels: a 40 kg unit does not get itself up to a third floor.
  • Protection against overload, low oil and automatic shut-off when out of fuel.
  • Technical service with a presence in Spain and a warranty of at least two years.
  • Noise declared at 7 metres, not at one metre.

Maintenance: so it starts on the day you need it

  • Oil change at the first 5 hours and afterwards as per the manual (every 50 hours or once a year).
  • Fresh petrol: no fuel that is six months old. Stabiliser if it is going to sit unused.
  • Start it for 15 minutes once a month, with some load plugged in.
  • Spark plug and air filter checked once a year.
  • Never store it with a full tank for months without stabiliser.

Mistakes that cost you dearly

  • Male-to-male cable to the home consumer unit. It is the fastest way to electrocute a worker who is repairing the grid on your street. You connect to the consumer unit with a transfer switch; if you do not have one, use extension leads to the appliances.
  • Buying 5 kW just in case. It uses more, sounds louder and always runs below its optimal load: it gets dirty and wears out sooner.
  • Not calculating the peak. The generator stalls just as the fridge starts.
  • Using old petrol. The engine will not start and you blame the equipment.
  • Skipping the first oil change. The first five hours are what decide the life of the engine.
  • Putting it in the garage. See above: it is the number one cause of CO deaths during blackouts.

Verdict

For a household that wants to get through a one- to three-day blackout without losing the fridge, the router or communications, the target is an inverter with 2,000 W continuous, electric start and an 8-10 litre tank. If you also want a boiler, microwave or power tools, 3,000-3,500 W. Below 1,500 W you will come up short as soon as a fridge kicks in; above 5 kW you will be paying for noise and fuel you do not use.

And remember what a generator does not solve: without water, without a first-aid kit, without a portable light and without a radio, there is no plan. The complete list, with the buying order and what you already have at home, is in the blackout kit and in the basic 72-hour kit.
