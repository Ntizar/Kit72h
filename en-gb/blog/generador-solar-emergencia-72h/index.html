Emergency solar generator: how much runtime it gives and 5 mistakes (2026)

📅 2026-10-01 · ⏱ 11 min min read

Three days without power is not an exotic scenario in Spain: it is what happens when a storm leaves an entire neighborhood without supply, when the grid goes out of kilter in the middle of a cold snap, or when roadworks cut a cable. Protección Civil kept the alert for showers and storms on the peninsular Mediterranean coast and the Balearic Islands in place on 16 October 2026, and autumn storms are, year after year, the most frequent cause of local electricity cuts. What decides whether that night is uncomfortable or unpleasant is not the blackout: it is whether you had somewhere to plug in the hallway light, the mobile phone and the fridge.

An emergency solar generator solves exactly that: it turns sunlight into electricity stored in a battery, with no noise, no fumes and no fuel to store. Below is the full calculation —how much energy you really need, how much battery and how many panels are required— and the mistakes that leave many people with a device that cannot even run the fridge.

1. What a solar generator is (and what it is not)

It is worth getting the names right, because shops use them as if they were synonyms and they are not:

  • Folding solar panel: it only generates electricity while the sun is shining on it. Without a battery it stores nothing.
  • External battery or portable power station: it stores energy and delivers it through an AC outlet, USB or 12 V. It does not charge itself: it needs someone to charge it.
  • Solar generator: the two together —panel + battery— in a kit that works from the first minute. This is what we are talking about here.
  • Petrol or diesel generator: it burns fuel to produce power. It works at night and under clouds, but it makes noise and smoke and needs refueling.

The practical difference is simple: solar does not depend on an external supply that runs out; as long as there is sun it keeps charging, and it can stay set up on the balcony for weeks. In exchange, its output depends on the sky. If you want to see solar versus fuel head to head, the comparison between a generator and a portable power station breaks down scenarios, noise and running costs; and if the solar route already suits you, the post on portable solar panels covers the three types and the buying mistakes.

2. The calculation that matters: watt-hours

The whole decision comes down to a single figure: the watt-hours (Wh) each device consumes each time you use it, and how many Wh fit in the battery. Nothing more. Power (W) tells you how much it uses while running; capacity (Wh) tells you how long it lasts.

DeviceTypical useApproximate consumption
Mobile phoneOne full chargeAround 10–15 Wh
LaptopWorking for 4 hoursBetween 40 and 60 Wh per charge
4G/Wi-Fi routerOne hour switched onAround 10 Wh
LED bulbOne night of useBetween 5 and 10 Wh

These are rule-of-thumb estimates, not laboratory measurements: your laptop may use considerably more if it has a graphics card. The idea to hold on to is the order of magnitude —charging a mobile phone costs a tiny fraction of what heating water costs—, because that is where the battery size comes from.

3. How much battery you need by scenario

With the table above, setting yourself up for the night is arithmetic. Three real scenarios, with no extra gear:

72-hour scenarioWhat it supportsRecommended capacity
Basic survivalMobile phones, flashlights, radio, router for short periodsBetween 300 and 600 Wh
Home in remote modeThe above plus laptop, LED lighting and portable fridgeBetween 1,000 and 1,500 Wh
High autonomyThe above plus tools, a small pump and continuous chargingAbove 2,000 Wh

The temptation is to buy the biggest battery “just in case” and end up with no panels, which is the mistake in point 5. Remember that the battery produces nothing: it only stores. A 12 Wh mobile phone charged four times a day is about 50 Wh per day; a portable fridge that runs in cycles can consume in one day what three mobile phones use in a week. If your priority is the fridge —for medicines or for food from the home fridge—, prioritize capacity over extras.

4. How many panels and how long they take to charge

Panels are sized with a rough but reliable rule: in good conditions, a 100 W panel delivers around 300–400 Wh on a full day of sun (direct light from morning to afternoon, at a reasonable angle). In other words, three effective hours of sun is the usual reference point for the calculation. From there:

PanelApproximate daily outputWhat it is for
20–40 WAbout 60–140 WhMobile phone, flashlights, radio. Small, steady charging
100 WBetween 300 and 400 WhHome in remote mode: mobile phones, LED lights, router, some fridge use
200 WBetween 600 and 800 WhDaily laptop use and fridge with margin
400 W (or two 200 W panels)More than 1,000 WhLarge batteries and quick recharges between storms

The sky is in charge, and in autumn and winter it is even more so: with drizzle or overcast sky, output falls to a fraction of the nominal figure —often to half or less— and the days are shorter. AEMET is the official source for knowing what sky to expect this week; Red Eléctrica de España (REE) publishes how the electricity system is behaving. The practical rule: size for the worst day of the year, not for the best day in August. If you live in the north or in a foggy area, add 50% more panel to whatever the calculation gave you.

5. What it powers and what it does not, without any misleading claims

This is the part that many product sheets keep quiet about. The short list:

  • Yes, with room to spare: mobile phones, tablets, laptops, flashlights and LED bulbs, radio, router, security cameras, battery chargers, hand tools, fans.
  • Yes, with a tight margin: portable fridge (in cycles), capsule coffee machine, iron, small microwave —anything that heats water uses a lot, a very great deal.
  • No, forget it: ceramic hob and oven, electric water heater, air conditioning, resistive heating, heat pump, hairdryer. An electric water heater can use in half an hour what the entire battery stores for three days.
  • With a separate calculation: well water pump, soldering iron, workshop compressor. They can be started with large batteries, but you have to calculate it device by device and check the start-up surges.

6. The five most common mistakes

  1. Buying only a battery, without panels. A portable power station without a panel is an expensive brick: you charge it at home and when the power goes out you use it once. If you cannot pay for both, start with the panel.
  2. Counting on August sun to get through a November blackout. Sizing is done for the worst day, not the best.
  3. Measuring in watts instead of watt-hours. “It has 1,000 W” does not say how long it lasts; “it has 1,000 Wh” does.
  4. Leaving the battery discharged in the storage room. Lithium batteries lose charge on their own: recharge it every three months and leave it at half charge if you are not going to touch it for a long time.
  5. Buying by the number of outlets rather than by power. Eight sockets that only accept light loads are no use for anything that heats.

And a sixth that is pure foresight: not buying anything. The difference between an afternoon blackout and a three-day one is almost never money; it is having looked at it in advance.

7. Setting it up in five steps

  1. Place the panel in the sun, facing south and at the angle specified by the manufacturer; on a window or balcony it works, but it performs less well.
  2. Connect the panel to the battery with the appropriate cable (panels have MC4 connectors; the battery comes with its own or you buy it separately).
  3. Wait for the battery to accept charge: with good light it starts filling within minutes.
  4. Connect your devices to the battery —AC outlets for 220 V items, USB for small devices— and switch off what you are not using.
  5. Complete the cycle every day: take the panel out into the sun in the morning, leave the battery in place and note down how much you consumed. With three nights of records you know whether you have spare capacity or not enough.

So that the night has light without burning through the battery, switch off what you do not need and turn on only two or three LEDs: a hallway LED bulb uses in an hour what a mobile phone uses in minutes.

8. Safety: what you need to know before plugging anything in

The good thing about the solar route is that it burns nothing inside the home: there is no carbon monoxide, no smoke and no fire risk from combustion. Fuel generators, by contrast, must never run in an enclosed space or near ventilation grilles —which is why the carbon monoxide detector is part of the same conversation.

The points you do need to watch with solar equipment:

  • Water and exposed connections: bring the panel in when it really rains and protect the sockets. Wet connectors corrode and stop charging properly.
  • Heat and ventilation: the battery copes better at a cool room temperature with airflow; do not put it under the bed or in a closed cupboard while charging.
  • Supervised charging while people are at home, as with any device that contains lithium.
  • Zero noise, zero red tape: because it makes no noise, you do not impose noise on the community. If you end up using a fuel generator as backup, check the noise rules of the residents' association before starting it.

9. What to check before paying anything

Buying checklist, in order of importance:

  • Real capacity in Wh, not mAh (mAh at 5 V is not the same as at 12 V, and shops play with that).
  • Output power in W —enough for what you want to plug in—, and if the manufacturer lists start-up surges, check them.
  • Battery type: lithium with LFP chemistry lasts many more cycles than others; for occasional emergency use, whichever you have at hand and well looked after.
  • Solar charging input on the same connectors as your panels, without improvised adapters.
  • Warranty and spare parts availability in Spain, not just an online shop.
  • Real weight: if it weighs more than you want to carry up to the balcony, you will not use it.

10. How this fits into a 72-hour plan

Light is only one piece. The recommendation set out in official guides —three days of self-sufficiency without basic services— is the axis around which the whole site is built, and there electricity sits alongside water, food, documents and communication. Secretaría General de Protección Civil y Emergencias publishes preventive recommendations by scenario, and the European Commission's Directorate-General for European Civil Protection and Humanitarian Aid Operations (DG ECHO) sets out the European approach to citizen preparedness; on the official sources page you will find them all linked in detail.

The sensible order, if you are starting from zero: cover communication and information first (mobile phone with battery and radio), then light, then cold and food. Summary in three lines

The calculation: aim for 500 Wh if you only want mobile phones and light, and 1,500 Wh if you want a fridge and a laptop. Recharging: 100 W of panel gives around 300–400 Wh per day in good sun, and half or less on a cloudy day. Discipline: battery charged and panel tested before it is needed, not during. With that, a three-day blackout becomes a day of working in the shade of the balcony.
