Emergency connectivity: Starlink, radio and satellite (2026)

📅 2026-10-01 · ⏱ 7 min min read

When the power grid goes down, the internet is the first thing to go. Then mobile coverage. In a DANA, a prolonged blackout or an earthquake, the three most widely used systems —WiFi, mobile and fibre— can fail at the same time. And without communication, there is no coordination, no information and no help.

This is where serious preparedness separates itself from improvisation. It is not about buying the most expensive gear: it is about having three independent systems that work autonomously. If one fails, the other two remain operational. If two fail, the third holds up.

This guide covers the three layers of emergency connectivity, from the easiest (Starlink) to the most robust (HF radio), with products, costs and when to use each one.

Layer 1: Satellite internet — Starlink

Starlink is the closest thing to terrestrial broadband internet that exists. It works from anywhere on the planet with a clear view of the sky, without fibre infrastructure or cell towers.

How does it work?

A flat terminal (the 'Dishy') is placed on a roof, balcony or garden and points to SpaceX's satellite constellation orbiting at an altitude of 550 km. The terminal draws between 50 and 100W in normal operation (5-10W in standby), so a lithium battery or a 200W solar panel keeps it running indefinitely.

Emergency specifications

  • Speed: 50-200 Mbps download, 10-20 Mbps upload (Gen 3)
  • Latency: 30-80 ms (identical to fibre in practical use)
  • Coverage: Global in areas with a clear view of the sky — Spain is covered across most of its territory
  • Installation: 5 minutes, no tools required
  • Power consumption: 50-100W (running) · 5-10W (standby)
  • Weather resistance: Resistant to winds of up to 120 km/h, rain, snow and hail

When should you use Starlink in an emergency?

Starlink is ideal when you need real internet — video calls, downloading documents, access to emergency systems — and no terrestrial infrastructure is available. It is especially useful for:

  • Emergency shelters or coordination points that need connectivity
  • Command centres that need to transmit information
  • Households in rural areas without fibre or 4G coverage
  • Evacuation to an area where the network has gone down

Limitation: The terminal is large (57 x 57 x 3 cm, 4.5 kg). It is not portable in an emergency backpack. It requires a fixed location with a clear view of the sky.

Layer 2: Cellular connectivity — redundancy

Mobile is the most widely used communication system. And that is why it is also the most vulnerable: a single operator going down leaves everyone disconnected. The key is to have two different operators and a router that manages them.

Industrial 4G/5G router with dual SIM

An industrial router such as the Peplink MAX BR, Gargoyle or similar accepts two SIMs from different operators (e.g. Movistar + Orange). If one tower goes down, the router switches to the other automatically. They also offer:

  • WAN/LAN: Ethernet ports to connect WiFi routers or a switch
  • VPN: you can connect your home router to a remote point
  • PoE: powered over network cable, ideal for roof installation
  • External antennas: SMA connectors for high-gain antennas
  • Web monitoring: dashboard to view network status, battery and signal

High-gain panel antenna

In rural areas, 4G signal can be weak but usable if the antenna is correctly aimed at the nearest tower. An 18-23 dBi panel antenna improves signal by 10 to 20 dB.

Procedure:

  1. Install the antenna on a 3-5 metre mast on the roof or facade
  2. Aim it at the nearest tower (apps such as CellMapper show towers on a map)
  3. Connect the coaxial cable (LMR-400, low loss) to the 4G/5G router
  4. Configure the router with the two SIMs

Layer 3: Satellite messaging — the last resort

When everything goes down — Starlink off due to lack of power, router without signal, and no radio at all — the satellite messenger is the last system still working. It does not require terrestrial infrastructure or a clear view of the sky to send text messages.

Garmin inReach Mini 2

The best-known satellite communication device for emergencies. It works on the Iridium network (global coverage, including the poles). It offers:

  • Two-way messages by SMS or to predefined contacts
  • Global SOS (connects to rescue coordination centres)
  • Integrated GPS with real-time location
  • Battery life of 2-3 days in normal mode (weeks in power-saving mode)
  • Water-resistant (IPX7), drop-resistant
  • Smartphone app: send messages from your phone without the device

Cost: ~350-450 EUR device + 30-50 EUR/month data plan

Zoleo

Alternative with a web dashboard: you can see your family's location in real time from any browser. It works on Iridium and Globalstar networks (dual network for a higher chance of connection).

Cost: ~300-400 EUR device + 30-50 EUR/month plan

When should you use the satellite messenger?

It is the device that should never be the only one, but it should always be in your evacuation backpack. It is used to:

  • Send an SOS signal when there is no other means
  • Communicate your location to family when you are lost
  • Confirm to family that you are OK when connectivity is restored
  • Receive emergency alerts (on some models)

Layer 4: Radio — the system that does not fail

Radio is the oldest, cheapest and most reliable communication system. It does not depend on satellites, it does not need infrastructure, and it does not become congested with traffic. When everything else fails, radio keeps working.

Professional PMR446 walkie-talkies

PMR446 walkie-talkies are the most basic and accessible communication system. They do not require a licence, work on 16 predefined channels and have a range of 2 to 5 km in open terrain (less in the city).

A set of 4 professional walkie-talkies (Motorola Talkabout or similar) costs ~100-200 EUR and is shared among family members. They are useful for:

  • Coordinating family evacuation (who goes where, when)
  • Communication at home during an emergency (between floors)
  • Coordination with neighbours or nearby rescue teams
  • Communication in areas with no mobile signal (even if only 2 km)

Specifications: 16 channels, 0.5 W power (the legal maximum for PMR446 without a licence), IP67 (water-resistant), LCD, VOX (hands-free), LED lights.

Amateur HF radio (CETIC licence)

Amateur radio is the deepest layer of emergency connectivity. With a shortwave antenna and an HF radio, you can communicate with any point on the planet via ionospheric propagation. It does not need any infrastructure at all.

To get started:

  • Licence: free exam at CETIC (Telecommunications Industry Testing Centre). You can prepare with free online material.
  • Radio: Icom IC-7100 or Yaesu FT-710 as a starting point (~600-1200 EUR)
  • Antenna: shortwave or dipole antenna (100-300 EUR)
  • Range: local (VHF) → regional (HF daytime) → international (HF night)

Amateur radio operators actively collaborate with Protección Civil in Spain through REDVOL (Volunteer Emergency Telecommunications Network). If you have a licence and want to help, there are local groups in every region.

Triple redundancy strategy

Professional preparedness is not based on a single system, but on triple redundancy: three independent systems that work autonomously. If one fails, the other two remain operational. If two fail, the third holds up.

Layer System Total cost Independence When it fails
1 Starlink (satellite) ~500 EUR + 50 EUR/month No terrestrial infrastructure No power / no clear view of the sky
2 Industrial 4G/5G router (2 SIMs) ~400-800 EUR + 80 EUR/month Alternative operator Towers down + no power
3 Satellite messenger ~350 EUR + 30 EUR/month Independent satellite network No battery
4 PMR446 walkie-talkies ~100-200 EUR Zero infrastructure No battery / limited range
5 Amateur HF radio ~600-1200 EUR No infrastructure at all Requires a licence + skill

The minimum functional budget

The essentials for decent connectivity when networks go down:

  • PMR446 walkie-talkies (4 units): ~150 EUR
  • Industrial 4G/5G router + 1 panel antenna: ~600 EUR
  • Satellite messenger (Garmin inReach): ~350 EUR
  • Minimum total: ~1.100 EUR (without subscriptions)

With Starlink it rises to ~1.600 EUR initially + ~80 EUR/month in subscriptions, but you get real broadband internet.

What you do not need to buy (yet)

Amateur HF radio is the most powerful layer but requires training and a licence. It is not essential for most households. If you are interested, start with PMR446 walkie-talkies and the satellite messenger; HF radio is a learning path you can begin studying once you have the basic systems covered.
