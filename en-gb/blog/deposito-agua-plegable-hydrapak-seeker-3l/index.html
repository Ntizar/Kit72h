HydraPak Seeker 3L and 10 L: the collapsible water container that actually deserves a place in your kit

There comes a point when putting together an emergency kit stops being a list and becomes a problem of space. The food goes in. The radio goes in. The torch goes in. And then comes the water, and everything falls apart: three litres per person per day is nine litres for 72 hours, which in 1.5-litre bottles is six bottles per person. Six bottles that take up room, that weigh something, that rattle with every step and that, empty, still take up the same as when they are full.

The 3-litre HydraPak Seeker attacks that problem from the only angle that works: it takes the container out of the problem. It is a flexible 92-gram reservoir that fills up when there is water and flattens down until it fits in a pocket when there isn't. It is not a hiker's gadget: it is the piece that turns “I carry water” into “I can carry water and, when it runs out, I can go and get more”.

What exactly it is (with the figures from the manufacturer's spec sheet)

The Seeker is a water bag, not a bottle. That changes everything else. These are the figures HydraPak gives and which are worth having to hand before deciding:

  • Capacity: 3 litres (100 ounces).
  • Empty weight: 92 grams. Less than a phone.
  • Dimensions when full: 37 × 14 cm, flat. It stands upright in the side pocket of almost any backpack.
  • Material: TPU with radio-frequency welded seams, plus polypropylene and nylon in the handle and reinforcements. BPA- and PVC-free.
  • Opening: 42 mm, with a screw cap attached to the bag (so it doesn't get lost) and a coated canvas handle for pouring.
  • Compatibility: 42 mm screw-on filters — the Katadyn BeFree and the brand's own filter cap. With the Plug-N-Play cap and a 28 mm adapter (sold separately) it can be used as a bladder with a tube.
  • Temperature: it can be frozen and takes hot water up to 60 °C. It is not a thermos and it won't take boiling water.
  • Extras: two side attachment points for hanging it or setting up gravity filtration, external volume markings and a carry bag included.

Reference price: between 20 and 30 € depending on finish and shop. For what it solves, it is one of the most cost-effective purchases in the kit.

Why 3 litres is the figure that fits

Spanish self-protection guidelines work with two numbers: 1.5 litres per person per day just for drinking and 3 litres per person per day if you also cook with that water. Over 72 hours that is between 4.5 and 9 litres per person. A 3-litre reservoir doesn't cover the whole scenario, and that is exactly what makes it useful: it covers one full day for one person with margin, or a day of drinking for two.

That size fits the real logic of the 72-hour kit, which is not “carry all the water” but carry water for the critical stretch and have a way to top it up. The big reserve stays at home, in 8-litre jerrycans you are not going to move. The Seeker is the part that goes out with you: to the car, to the shelter, to the distribution point, to the public fountain that is still working when the tap at home isn't.

If you want the full calculation of how much water a household needs, it is set out in the guide to how much water to store and how to store it. Here the question is different: how do you move it.

The five reasons it beats a jerrycan

1. Empty, it takes up no space. It is the main reason and it doesn't need much explaining: an empty 5-litre jerrycan is still a 5-litre jerrycan. The empty Seeker folds, flattens and goes in its bag, which takes up less than a wallet. In an evacuation backpack, where every litre of volume competes with clothing and the first-aid kit, that gap is gold.

2. It adapts to the space there is, not the other way round. Because it isn't rigid, it moulds to the shape of the pack, goes into a side pocket, hangs from the carry handle and doesn't create that uncomfortable pressure on your back that a rigid bottle does when the backpack is full. In the car boot the same applies: it slots into any nook between boxes.

3. It turns the source into a tap. Here is what almost nobody values until they use it: the 42 mm cap takes screw-on filters. You fill the bag in a river, in an irrigation channel or in a doubtful tank, screw on the filter and drink straight from it or by gravity with the bag hung from the attachment points. It is the difference between carrying water and being able to make it. The filters that fit without an adapter are the 42 mm screw-on ones, such as the Katadyn BeFree. If the scenario is a flood with the supply cut off or contaminated, that capability stops being a mountain extra and becomes the centre of the plan. More on this in the comparison of water purifiers for emergencies.

4. It does more than drinking. Three litres of hot water (up to 60 °C) inside a sleeping bag is a full-body heat pack. Frozen, it is a block of ice that keeps a cooler or a food box cold. With the cap closed it works as an emergency shower hung from a branch. And when you get back home, it will do the washing-up in a blackout without using up the drinking reserve. A jerrycan does one thing; this does five.

5. It takes the abuse. TPU with radio-frequency welded seams is the same material used in high-end hydration bladders. It is not a disposable plastic bag: it can be frozen, filled with hot water, stood on and stored wet without bursting. The brand gives a lifetime warranty against manufacturing defects, which for a 25 € product says a fair amount about what they expect of it.

The PET bottle is cheap reserve that gets rotated every six months. The bladder with tube is for drinking without stopping on the move, if you do sport. Where it falls short (and how to fix it)

No tool is perfect, and this one less than most. What you need to know before buying it:

  • At first it tastes of plastic. It is the first thing people who use it say and it is true: new TPU leaves a taste in the first few fills. It is fixed with two washes in warm water with a little bicarbonate of soda, leaving it full for a couple of hours and drying it open. After that it disappears. Anyone who doesn't wash it before the first use will regret it at the worst possible moment.
  • Full, it doesn't stand up on its own. It is flexible: if you leave it upright on the table, it topples over. It isn't a real problem (you hang it from the handle or lean it against the backpack), but if you were expecting a bottle that stands upright, it isn't one.
  • It is not for drinking on the move. Without the Plug-N-Play cap and the 28 mm adapter, you have to stop, open it and pour. If your plan is to run with it, buy a bladder with a tube. If your plan is an emergency kit, this detail is irrelevant.
  • The 42 mm cap doesn't fit 28 mm filters without an adapter. Before buying the filter, check the thread: Katadyn BeFree and HydraPak's own caps yes; many hand-crank pumps, no.
  • It is not a storage container. Leaving water inside for weeks is asking for trouble: it picks up taste and grows biofilm. Empty it, dry it open and put it away.
  • In a long blackout, three litres run out. It is a transport reservoir, not the reserve. The reserve is the jerrycans at home. Anyone who confuses the two comes up short on day two.

How I set it up in a 72-hour kit

There is no need to complicate it. This is the setup that makes sense, from the small to the large:

  1. At home: two 8-litre water jerrycans per person, rotated every six months, plus a water purifier. That is the store and it isn't touched except in an emergency.
  2. In the evacuation backpack: the empty Seeker (you won't notice 92 g) and a 42 mm screw-on filter. Total weight committed: under 200 g, and the ability to drink from any reasonably clean source.
  3. In the car: a second empty Seeker in the glovebox or under the seat, with two litres of reserve in PET bottles that get rotated. The reservoir is for refilling at a petrol station or a fountain when things drag on.
  4. The routine: before each risk season (the autumn of DANAs, the summer of wildfires), check it over: bag dry, cap clean, filter within its service life, and a fill test to rule out pinholes.

If you are building the kit from scratch, the right order is water first and gadgets afterwards. The bigger brother: the 10-litre Seeker

If your problem isn't moving water but keeping it still in one place, the same bag exists in 10 litres. It is 261 grams empty and 49 cm tall, and it comes with the Plug-N-Play cap and a camp tap (Camp Tap): you hang it from a beam, a door frame or the tent flysheet and serve water with one hand, without lifting anything. That detail is what separates it from a jerrycan: a jerrycan has to be tipped, this one stays hanging and you just open the tap.

The numbers match the official recommendation. Ten litres is almost exactly the three litres per person per day that the guidelines ask for over 72 hours: for one person it covers the full three days and for a family of three it covers the first day without touching the rest of the reserve. Full it weighs 10 kilos, so it stays where you put it; empty it takes up less than a folder and, properly dry, it doesn't pick up smells between emergencies.

It can be frozen, it takes hot water up to 60 °C, it is TPU with no BPA or PVC and it shares the range's filter compatibility. In a flat, its natural place is the bottom of the cupboard: you fill it on the day of the warning, hang it in the kitchen or the bathroom and empty it when the warning has passed.

Who it's for and who it isn't

Buy it if: you have or are building a 72-hour kit, an evacuation backpack, a car kit or a mountain kit; you live in a flood or wildfire area or somewhere with supply cuts; or you simply want to be able to take water from a source without lugging bottles around. For 20-30 € it is one of the best weight-to-usefulness ratios in the whole kit.

Don't buy it if: you are looking to store water at home (you need rigid jerrycans), you want to drink without stopping while you run (you need a bladder with a tube), or you expect a bottle that stands upright on the table. Neither if the plan is to boil water inside it: it isn't a pan.

The verdict

The Seeker 3L isn't the flashiest product in an emergency kit and it is probably the one you will use most often when there is no emergency at all: a hike, a day at the beach, a car trip, refilling at a fountain. That is precisely the argument in its favour. A kit that only gets used in catastrophes rusts away in the cupboard; one that leaves the house now and then gets known, gets tested and is to hand on the day you need it.
